Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline. On the rows whose English was never in the SFT text (\clean{}, the only subset without known exposure) RL gains over SFT on every benchmark with an interval above zero: FLORES+ +6.62 $\ci{5.83}{7.24}$ (48 sentences $\times$ 45 languages), NTREX-128 +4.09, SmolSent +7.06, GlotStoryBook +8.26, MAFAND-MT +3.66 and SALT +2.31 $\ci{0.85}{3.57}$; the gain is as large or larger than on \nopair{} rows for FLORES+, NTREX-128, SmolSent and GlotStoryBook, equal for MAFAND-MT and smaller for SALT (and for IbomMT and FLORES-MOZ, Table~\ref{tab:exposuresize}). On FLORES+ devtest (\nopair{} rows) RL raises the macro chrF++ over 45 languages from 28.7 to 34.1 (+5.42 $\ci{5.22}{5.61}$) and spBLEU from 9.2 to 12.4 (+3.19), and all 45 languages improve. The exposure raises the \emph{levels} of both models (all rows minus \clean{} rows on FLORES+: +3.8 chrF++ for SFT-final and +2.6 for RL-final; SmolSent +6.4 and +4.6), not the conclusion. The same holds on seven further benchmarks, including IbomMT, whose Ibibio and Efik sets lie outside the usual benchmarks; its Efik half is affected by the reward calibration (Efik +12.70, unaffected Ibibio +9.37), so IbomMT is not an independent result for Efik. No RL language is worse on FLORES+; the only chrF++ regression whose interval excludes zero on a \nopair{} subset is Luganda on SALT ($-2.62$ $\ci{-5.17}{-0.33}$, 81 sentences), and the smallest gains (+0.4 to +1.1) are the high-resource languages Sesotho, Zulu, Xhosa, Afrikaans, Somali and Swahili, where several intervals include zero.

\paragraph{Per-language gains and weak languages.} Gains are largest where the SFT model was weakest (Figure~\ref{fig:gain}): +11.22 for the two consensus-only languages, against +4.95 for the 40 QE-validated languages and +1.26 for Afrikaans and Malagasy. The largest single gains, over the 44 languages that also have an NLLB row, are Kabuverdianu +14.2, Kabiye +14.1, Kamba +12.1, Luo +11.9, Nuer +11.4 and Kanuri +11.0 chrF++ (mean +5.35, median +4.38; Table~\ref{tab:skewlangs}); on the 48 never-seen sentences they are as large (Kabuverdianu +15.2, Luo +14.1, Kabiye +13.0, Nuer +12.7, Kamba +12.1). Part of this is repair, not new knowledge: SFT finished only 34\%, 51\% and 76\% of the Kabiye, Nuer and Kabuverdianu outputs (RL 100\%), Kanuri has no SFT data, and in the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ab2} the gain on sentences every system finished is much smaller. Kabuverdianu is both the largest gain over SFT and the largest remaining gap to NLLB-200 3.3B (21.0). On the primary benchmark of each language the 13 consensus-only languages that have one improved by +7.69 $\ci{7.30}{8.07}$ and the 9 blend languages by +7.93, against +4.97 for the 42 QE-validated ones (a coverage summary, not like-for-like): languages whose reward contained \emph{no learned metric} improved at least as much as the validated ones. The Ge'ez-script languages improve by about +4 (Amharic 14.5 to 18.9, Tigrinya 9.4 to 13.8) and remain far behind NLLB (Section~\ref{sec:cmp}). Kanuri has no SFT data and rises from 3.9 to 14.8, but GlotLID recognises none of its output and its finished rate rises from 38\% to 99.9\%; we read this as a stopping effect, not as evidence that RL generalises to a language without data.

\paragraph{Part of the gain is repair of unfinished outputs.} A looping completion is scored as it stands, and the finished rate rises from 90.4\% to 100\%. At a 320-token budget the same comparison is 30.4 to 34.1 (+3.70): about 1.7 of the 5.4 points are the SFT model's runaway tail, and the remainder still contains the 10\% of SFT sentences that loop inside 320 tokens. Learning to stop is part of what RL buys, and our data cannot fully separate it from translation quality (the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ab2} shows the same pattern more sharply).

\paragraph{When the gain was made.} On a 760-sentence dev set (19 languages) the macro chrF++ rises from 39.65 for SFT-final to 41.00 after the first 50 steps of phase~1 and 41.77 at step 100 of phase~2, and then stays between 41.6 and 42.0: over the 1,433 steps and about 900,000 sampled completions of phase~2c it moves from 41.89 to 41.88. The sentence-level gain was thus in place after roughly the first million completions and later stages changed nothing at sentence level.

\paragraph{Sentence by sentence.} On 161,272 English-to-X sentences of the eight benchmarks (66 languages with at least 20 \nopair{} sentences) RL-final's mean sentence chrF++ rises from 27.7 to 31.1, and it is preferred over SFT-final by chrF++ in 63.7\% $\ci{63.5}{64.0}$ of the non-tied sentences, by reference-based AfriCOMET in 70.0\% and by SSA-COMET (not in RL-final's reward) in 70.4\%; the chrF++ gain is significant in 65 of 66 languages and negative in none, and the preference is above 50\% in every benchmark and reward group. This is agreement of metrics with human references, not a human evaluation, and the metrics agree on the winner of a sentence in only 64--73\% of cases.

\subsection{Comparison with other systems}\label{sec:cmp}
% Long paper: Section 6.2, 6.8, Appendix G; Table t_cmp_main, t_cmp_wins
\textbf{Headline.} Over the 42 languages without the two Ge'ez-script languages (an outlier with an untested cause, reported next to the rest in Table~\ref{tab:nogeezflores}, not left out as unimportant), RL-final is 3.32 chrF++ and 0.043 AfriCOMET behind NLLB-200 3.3B and 1.13 and 0.028 behind the 600M model; over all 44 languages 3.85 and 0.050, and 1.69 and 0.035. The mean is skewed by a few languages: the median per-language gap to the 3.3B model is 2.78 (3.17 over all 44), and Kabuverdianu (21.0, kept in), Amharic and Tigrinya carry 30\% of the summed gap (Ge'ez script alone 17.7\%, Kabuverdianu alone 12.4\%; without the three, mean 2.89 and median 2.48; Table~\ref{tab:skewsummary}). On the 43 languages NLLB-200 covers (Amharic and Tigrinya included), RL-final reaches 34.7 chrF++ $\ci{34.6}{34.8}$ against 36.4 for NLLB-200-distilled-600M and 38.4 for NLLB-200 3.3B, and 0.620 AfriCOMET against 0.654 and 0.669 (Table~\ref{tab:cmp}, Appendix~\ref{app:geez}). \textbf{The model does not beat NLLB:} on these 43 the paired deficit is 1.7 $\ci{1.6}{1.8}$ to the 600M and 3.7 $\ci{3.6}{3.8}$ to the 3.3B model; on \clean{} rows the gaps are 0.7 and 3.0 (0.70 and 3.16 over the 44 languages of the headline set), so the ordering does not depend on the exposure. RL-final is higher than the 600M model in 17 of 44 languages (7 clear wins, 19 clear losses, intervals not overlapping) and higher than the 3.3B model in 8 of 44 on chrF++ (Dyula, Umbundu, Kamba, Wolof, Kimbundu, Fon, Yoruba, Igbo; point estimates, 6 clear wins, 30 clear losses; on AfriCOMET 4 of 42: Fon, Igbo, Wolof, Yoruba). The nearest small competitor, TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B (trained on NLLB-3.3B translations), is clearly better on its 19 languages: 45.3 against 41.2 chrF++ (+4.1 $\ci{3.9}{4.2}$; RL-final is higher in none and clearly lower in 14) and 0.717 against 0.657 AfriCOMET. On 21 languages, RL-final's 39.1 (\nopair{} rows) lies above the published 31.1--38.6 of adapted 4--14B Gemma, Llama and Qwen models and a 50-language 4B Qwen variant reaches 41.7, but the published numbers are for all 1,012 sentences while our rows contain the English of 95\% of them, and a comparison on \clean{} rows is not possible because their per-sentence outputs are not available (an earlier draft set our 48 harder \clean{} sentences against their whole-devtest scores, which was not like for like): we claim neither superiority nor inferiority.

\paragraph{Honest losses: Ge'ez script.} The largest deficits to NLLB-200 3.3B (published FLORES-200 values) are Kabuverdianu ($-21.1$), Amharic (37.9 against 18.9, $-19.0$) and Tigrinya (24.8 against 13.8, $-11.0$); RL improved Amharic and Tigrinya by only +4.4 and +4.3. The tokenizer fragments these scripts (1.66 and 1.35 characters per Gemma token for Amharic and Tigrinya against 3.11 for Swahili; Appendix~\ref{app:cpu}) and the SFT data for them are comparatively small, but we have not tested what causes the gap and do not claim to have solved it. Without the two languages the gaps are smaller, not gone: AfriCOMET is 0.624 against 0.667 and 0.651 on the 40 other languages that have a score, and RL-final is below the 3.3B model in 34 of the 42 (Appendix~\ref{app:geez}).

\paragraph{Coverage.} The model supports 77 target languages (55 for NLLB-200-600M), 33 of them absent from FLORES-200; this is a coverage statement, not a quality statement, and we claim no priority for breadth. NLLB-200 (CC-BY-NC) and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM appear only as reference baselines.

\subsection{Controlled ablation: does the gain survive a weak SFT start?}\label{sec:ab2}
% Long paper: Section 8.4, Tables t_ablation, t_ablation_sets, Fig 11; ledger Part I
The observational comparison of RL gains with the (small) gains from added parallel data confounds many factors, so we ran a small controlled ablation (AB2) with a decision rule written and hashed before any run.
\paragraph{Design.} \emph{Weak start W0:} the first 3,500 steps of SFT re-run from \texttt{gemma-3-270m-it} on exactly the packs the real run drew, with the learning rate annealed over the shortened horizon: 0.459 B tokens, 12.7\% of SFT-final's (it is not a snapshot of the real run and may be less weak than its data suggest). \emph{Arm F}: RL from SFT-final (F0); \emph{arm W}: RL from W0; identical phase-2c recipe, prompts and seeds, 49 steps of 64 b64 prompts with a group of 16 (50,176 completions per arm), in four languages only (Yoruba, Kinyarwanda, Oromo, Lingala). \emph{Controls from W0:} S1, continued SFT with GPU time equal to one RL arm (367 steps, about 48 M tokens, one untuned recipe) and S1P, SFT on 3,136 sentences, as many as the RL prompt draws. The controls use the SFT windows of the main corpus and no NLLB-derived text. Endpoint: language-macro chrF++ on FLORES+ \nopair{} rows with paired bootstrap; confirmation on NTREX, MAFAND-MT, SmolSent, the sentences every system finished, and AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference (AfriCOMET-QE, the reward model, enters no verdict). The rule fixes preconditions (F0$-$W0 $\ge3$ chrF++; W0 finishes $\ge60\%$) and forbids the sentences ``RL replaces SFT'' unless $W_{RL}-F_{RL}$ contains 0 and ``$W_{RL}$ reaches F0'' unless $W_{RL}-F_0$ contains 0 or is above. The minimum detectable effect is 1.16 chrF++ for the difference of the gains and about 0.82 for RL against a control.

\paragraph{Result.} All preconditions hold (F0$-$W0 $=13.61$ $\ci{12.96}{14.29}$; W0 finishes 70.0\%). The pre-registered rule reads \textbf{support} for the claim that RL's gain survives a weak start: the interval of the weak-start gain is above zero, survival (gain$_W$/gain$_F$) is 1.29 $\ci{1.14}{1.45}$, and the gain is above zero on all three confirmation sets, on the finished-by-all sentences, in 4 of 4 languages and under both SSA-COMET and AfriCOMET with reference (Table~\ref{tab:ab2} and Figure~\ref{fig:ab2} in Appendix~\ref{app:abtables}). From the same weak start, RL (+7.36) beat both continued-SFT controls (+0.32 and $-1.01$) by +7.04 and +8.37 chrF++, and still does so on the finished-by-all sentences (+1.29, +1.45), in this configuration of 49 RL steps against 367 SFT steps on about 400 times as many training tokens as the RL prompts have source tokens; this says nothing about SFT with better data or a different schedule, which we did not try.

\paragraph{What it cannot show.} (1) RL does \emph{not} replace SFT: $W_{RL}$ stays 6.25 chrF++ below SFT-final, and the rule forbids both sentences above. (2) Much of the weak-start chrF++ gain is repair of unfinished output: the finished rate rises by 18.7 points (against 4.8 for arm F), and on the sentences every system finished the weak start gains \emph{less} in chrF++ (+1.13 against +2.23; difference $-1.10$ $\ci{-1.60}{-0.58}$), while AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference give it the larger gain; the ranking of who gains more depends on the metric. (3) It is one short b64 phase of 49 steps (2.9\% of the completions of the main run), not the production schedule; four languages (no Ge'ez language, no weak consensus-only language); one seed per arm and control, so run-to-run variance is not in any interval; the weak start is a re-created annealed SFT; one untuned control recipe; no human evaluation, no document-level evidence.

\subsection{Final run, science stage and long documents}\label{sec:final}
% Long paper: Sections 4.6, 4.7, 6.6, 6.7, 6.10; Tables t_longctx_human_chrf, t_longctx_failure_human; ledger Parts F, G, H, K
RL-final translates single-pass inputs of about 512 source tokens but loops or truncates beyond (greedy finished rate 98/97/92/65/42/31\% for b64 to b4096). A short supervised stage on AfriScience-MT \citep{abdulmumin2026afriscience} with replay and long-document distillation, interpolated with RL-final ($\alpha=0.7$, chosen by guardrails on dev sets because plain fine-tuning lowered the weak languages), and then a \emph{final RL run} of 401 steps on science and general prompts (the QE ensemble, anchors, long-document reward and the NLLB pseudo-reference signal of Section~\ref{sec:setup}; interrupted by two out-of-memory kills and resumed from step 250; wound down after step 350 by a pre-set stopping rule) moved the sentence-level development scores by at most +0.4 chrF++ (AfriScience dev 48.79 to 48.92) and left 97\% of the development papers finished against 93\% at the start: a stable continuation, not a further gain. Evaluated afterwards, the final checkpoint is level with RL-final on the general benchmarks: +0.23 chrF++ $\ci{0.19}{0.27}$ on FLORES+, but $-0.0016$ AfriCOMET and $-0.0017$ SSA-COMET (one run; the anchors of the final run were fitted on test splits of NTREX-128 and SmolSent, so differences there are not independent tests); it is preferred in 52\% of non-tied sentences by chrF++ and 47--48\% by the two learned metrics. On the AfriScience-MT test split it is +2.40 $\ci{2.25}{2.56}$ sentence chrF++ and +13.0 paper-level chrF above RL-final, but only +0.17 above the science-stage model it started from (paper level within noise): that gain comes from the supervised science stage, not from RL. We therefore keep RL-final as the headline model, and the claims of Sections~\ref{sec:sftrl} and \ref{sec:ab2} concern RL-final and its SFT start.
\paragraph{Long documents.} Table~\ref{tab:longctx} (Appendix~\ref{app:abtables}) shows what the science-stage and final-run checkpoints do that RL-final does not: they generate whole documents of 1,000--2,000 tokens natively, in one pass, and write the target language (chrF++ 39.6 and 27.7 at about 1,024 and 2,048 tokens against 5.9--14.9 for whole-document NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy, including the 2B model), but they omit about 12\% and 27\% of the source sentences. With a sentence-by-sentence wrapper (a way to compare with systems that cannot take long input) every system scores higher and NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy are 1--4 chrF++ \emph{ahead} of the final run. Better on native long input is therefore not better document translation, and we do not claim better document translation than other systems; the whole-document comparison is largely a statement about the baselines' input limits. An independent LLM judge on aligned units of the same documents agrees (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, Appendix~\ref{app:judge}): the final model's whole-document units score 25.8 of 100 against 67.0 for its wrapper, so native output finishes and writes the language but is not good translation. chrF++ against pseudo-references is much less harsh than the judge, which puts even the wrapper 19.9 points behind NLLB-200 3.3B; and the whole-document failure is not a Ge'ez effect (Yoruba 9.0 and Kinyarwanda 14.6 against Amharic 7.7; Appendix~\ref{app:geez}).

The final checkpoint converted to GGUF Q8\_0 reproduces the chrF++ of the GPU output ($-0.02$ $\ci{-0.22}{0.16}$; Table~\ref{tab:cpu} in Appendix~\ref{app:cpu}) in a 300 MB file with 1.2 GB of RAM at batch 32, and translates about 2.0 sentences per second on four server cores (0.97 at batch 1; 139 hours per million sentences; Figure~\ref{fig:cpu}). The 4-bit GGUF loses 1.4 chrF++ without being faster, and CTranslate2 int8 is faster at large batch (2.87 sentences per second) but loses 1.7. For reference, NLLB-200 3.3B with beam 4 scores 41.0 against 35.0 for ours on the same sentences at 0.48 sentences per second and 10.9 GB, and NLLB-600M with beam 4 scores 38.7 at 1.49 sentences per second; the decoders differ and the macro gap sits almost entirely in the two Ge'ez-script languages. NLLB-600M with greedy decoding is both faster (4.2 sentences per second) and 2.4 chrF++ better than our Q8\_0 on these sentences. A laptop speed of 1--2 sentences per second is an \emph{estimate} (0.5--1.0 times the measured speed), not a measurement. The tokenizer makes the cost language-dependent: Yoruba and Tigrinya sentences take about 1.6 times as long as Swahili ones, and the languages that cost most per sentence are those with the lowest chrF++ (Spearman correlation of characters per token with chrF++ 0.91 over 16 languages; an association, not a mechanism).

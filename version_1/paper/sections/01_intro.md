## 1 Introduction

Most African languages are served poorly by machine translation. Large systems cover a few dozen of them, and the community benchmarks that measure the rest are small. At the same time there is demand for models that run on a phone or a laptop without a network. We ask how far a *very small* model, Gemma 3 with 270 million parameters, can be pushed on English-to-African translation, and where the limits lie, when the training signal after supervised fine-tuning (SFT) comes from reinforcement learning (RL) with learned quality metrics instead of more parallel data.

The setting is demanding in three ways. First, the languages: 77 targets, from Swahili and Zulu (tens of millions of speakers, decent data) to Esan, Edo, Ghomala and Okpe, for which the only parallel text we could find is largely religious. For 23 of them no learned translation-quality metric exists at all. Second, the model: 270M parameters, of which 168M are the 262k-entry embedding table, so the transformer proper is tiny. Third, the length: we wanted the model to translate whole documents of up to 2,048 source tokens, which in a language with fragmented tokenisation means an output of 4,000 tokens or more.

RL with a quality-estimation (QE) reward is an obvious tool, but a reward that is a learned model is a target to be gamed, and the literature and our own corruption tests list the cheap exploits: copying the English source, appending text, repeating, answering in the wrong language, stopping early, or writing the same stock sentence for every prompt. Most of this paper is therefore about what surrounds the reward and about the measurements that motivated each safeguard. Whether the policy would have found these exploits without the safeguards our runs cannot say, because every run had the hard failure classes, and for the extra terms of the final run (anchors, ensemble, long-document reward) a stored-output check finds no demonstrated protective effect, only a design and some lower loop rates without a control run (Section 7.9). The paper tells the story in order, including the parts that did not work: a training phase that diverged, a detector with a length bug, a teacher model that could not teach, a science fine-tuning stage that erased what RL had gained until it was interpolated away, and a long-document goal that was met for RL-final only by changing the decoding strategy, and natively, with omissions, only after the science stage.

**Contributions.** To our knowledge, this is the first open 270M-parameter model fine-tuned with RL against an African-language quality estimator (a QE-ensemble reward) on prompts of up to 2,048 source tokens; the single-pass document result is narrower than that training range (Sections 6.4 and 6.10), and the statement concerns the training prompts, not the quality of document translation relative to other systems. Our literature search (about 28 queries on 2026-10-22 and 2026-10-23, Section 2) found no earlier work with this combination; Section 2 lists the closest prior work and what we do not claim. On breadth we claim only that, to our knowledge, it is the open translation model with the widest African target-language coverage below 1B parameters (77 languages, against 55 for NLLB-200-600M), and that it covers 33 African languages absent from FLORES-200 (17 of which we could not find in NLLB-200, MADLAD-400 or TranslateGemma); we do not claim to be the first or the only system with this breadth (Section 6.8).

1. *A reward design and its evidence* (Sections 4.3, 4.4, 7). A pessimistic QE ensemble (mean of two judges, minimum when they disagree by more than one reward unit), per-language shaping on human anchors, language-aware sentence alignment in which omissions are failed chunks, a consensus signal for languages without a metric, hard failure classes, constraint detectors calibrated on human text with measured false-positive rates, and a second reward for long documents. For each component we report the measurement that motivated it, including negative ones (a SSA-COMET copy exploit on corrupted references, a detector flagging 100% of long human documents, a consensus attractor in simulation), and in Section 7.9 what the extra terms of the final run did and did not do on stored outputs: no demonstrated protection against copying, entity loss or stop-word profile (RL-final did not show these failures), padding and repetition 2-4 pp lower than RL-final in the weak and consensus-only languages, and no control run that would let us attribute either result to the terms.
2. *Gains on 77 languages with a 270M model* (Sections 6.1-6.3). On FLORES+ the macro chrF++ rises by 5.4 (28.7 to 34.1, 95% interval [5.2, 5.6]; 45 of 45 languages improve), and by 6.6 [5.8, 7.2] on the 48 sentences per language whose English side was never in our training text; on the never-seen rows RL also gains over SFT on NTREX-128 (+4.1), MAFAND-MT (+3.7), SmolSent (+7.1), GlotStoryBook (+8.3) and SALT (+2.3), each with an interval above zero, while the levels of both models are inflated by the exposure of the test sets to our training and reward calibration (Section 6.9a). Gains are largest where the SFT model was weakest, including languages whose reward contained no learned metric (Kabuverdianu +14.2, Kabiye +14.1, Kamba +12.1, Luo +11.9, Nuer +11.4, Kanuri +11.0 chrF++ on FLORES+; mean +5.35, median +4.38 over 44 languages; part of the largest gains is repaired unfinished output and the controlled ablation of Section 8.4 shows a smaller gain on finished sentences). A sentence-by-sentence comparison with the SFT model on 161k sentences (Section 6.9) finds RL preferred in 63.7% (chrF++) and about 70% (reference-based AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET) of the sentences on which they differ, in 65 of 66 languages and every benchmark and group, with a check against reward hacking; it is evidence about agreement with human references under automatic metrics, not a human evaluation. An independent LLM judge, validated at Spearman about 0.5 against human ratings of other systems' output, also prefers RL-final on 1,570 FLORES+ sentences in eight languages (+4.0 of 100, 95% interval [3.2, 4.9]; higher in seven languages, level in Xhosa; Section 6.12). On identical sentences the model remains 3.32 chrF++ and 0.043 AfriCOMET behind NLLB-200 3.3B on the 42 languages without the two Ge'ez-script languages (our headline comparison; 3.85 and 0.050 on all languages) and 1.13 and 0.028 behind NLLB-200-distilled-600M (1.69 and 0.035 on all), while Amharic and Tigrinya, left out of the headline because they are an outlier with an untested cause, are 19.0 and 13.8 chrF++ against 38.0 and 24.7 for the 3.3B model (Section 6.8). The mean gap is skewed by a few languages (median 2.78 over the 42, 3.17 over all 44; Kabuverdianu, Amharic and Tigrinya carry 30% of the all-language gap), and Kabuverdianu, which stays in every set, is both the largest gain over SFT (+14.2) and the largest remaining gap to NLLB-200 3.3B (21.0). We make no claim of superiority or inferiority over the published 4-14B Afrique LLMs: their numbers are for all 1,012 sentences and their per-sentence outputs are not available (Section 6.2).
3. *An honest account of length* (Sections 4.2, 6.4, 6.10, 7.4). A curriculum of five length bins up to 2,048-token inputs with a chunked reward extended the single-pass translation range from about 256 to about 512 source tokens, but greedy whole-document translation of 1,024- and 2,048-token inputs still loops or truncates (finished 65% and 42%); translating sentence by sentence with the same model (a wrapper that we use to compare with systems that cannot take long input; our models generate the whole document natively), and repairing the 1% of unfinished sentences, gives 55.6-60.1 chrF++ against an NLLB reference for 1,024-4,096-token documents and about 52 chrF++ against human references at 2,048 tokens. We diagnose why the reward could not teach single-pass long translation and give a second reward that fixes its detectors. A benchmark against other systems (Section 6.10) tests the hypothesis that our model translates 2,048-token documents far better than others and finds it only partly true: after the supervised science stage (not the RL checkpoint) the model finishes and writes the target language on 1,000-2,000-token documents in a single pass (chrF++ 39.4-39.6 and 28.2-28.8 against human references, against 5.9-14.9 for NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM in whole-document mode), but it omits 13-30% of the sentences, and a sentence-by-sentence wrapper gives every system higher scores, with NLLB and TranslatePsy 1-4 chrF++ ahead of ours: better on native long input is not better document translation. An independent LLM judge on aligned units agrees and is sharper (Section 6.12): the final model's whole-document units score 25.8 of 100 against 67.0 for its sentence-by-sentence wrapper and 85.6 for NLLB-200 3.3B (five languages; 16.4% of the sampled sentences get no output).
4. *Where the gain comes from, why it stops, and a research question* (Sections 6.3, 8, 9, 10). The sentence-level gain was complete after roughly a million sampled completions; the next 0.9 million changed nothing at sentence level. We present four hypotheses for the plateau (capacity, reward alignment, data, measurement) with the evidence for each. We also pose the research question whether RL is a *data-efficient complement to SFT*, which our runs inform but do not answer: our runs show gains from RL (+5.4 chrF++ macro; about +4 for the Ge'ez-script languages Amharic and Tigrinya) against at most +0.6 from the parallel data we could add, with per-unit-of-data numbers, eight listed confounds and and a small controlled ablation (RL from a re-created weak SFT with 12.7% of the SFT tokens, four languages, 49 steps, one seed per arm, Section 8.4). In the ablation, RL from the weak start gained +7.36 chrF++ against +5.72 from SFT-final and beat two continued-SFT controls (+0.32 and -1.01), but it did not reach SFT-final (-6.25), and on sentences that every system finished the chrF++ gain from the weak start is the smaller one (+1.13 against +2.23), so we do not say that RL replaces SFT.
5. *Case studies* (Section 9). The Ge'ez-script languages, where the tokenizer, the SFT data and RL interact; the 23 consensus-only languages, where no learned metric exists and a 31B teacher did not help; and Urhobo, where an LLM-generated silver set serves as a detector check but not as evidence of quality.
6. *A like-for-like comparison with other systems* (Section 6.8). AfriCOMET is published for almost none of the baselines, so we re-scored the systems we could run (NLLB-200 600M and 3.3B, TranslatePsy-AfriSLM, our checkpoints) on identical sentences with one metric stack; we do not beat NLLB (headline: 3.32 chrF++ behind the 3.3B model over the 42 languages without Amharic and Tigrinya, 3.85 over all 44, the two Ge'ez-script languages reported next to it in the same table; medians 2.78 and 3.17, because a few languages carry the gap), and we state precisely which coverage claims we can and cannot make. A reward widening for a next round is calibrated but not used (Section 7.8).
7. *Tokenisation cost and CPU deployment* (Sections 3.3, 6.11, 10). The Gemma tokenizer needs 1.5-1.7 times as many output tokens for Yoruba and Tigrinya as for Swahili, which makes a sentence take about 1.6 times as long on a CPU, and the languages that cost most are those with the lowest chrF++; quantised to GGUF Q8\_0 the model loses nothing measurable against the GPU output (-0.02 chrF++, [-0.22, +0.16]) and translates about two sentences per second on four cores of a shared server (laptop speed: an estimate). NLLB-200 is shown as a reference only.
8. *Negative results and practical lessons* (Sections 4.5, 6.6, 7). A divergence of GRPO without standardised advantages, with the guards that fixed it; a padding detector whose false-positive rate depended on text length; a plain science fine-tuning stage that lowered the languages RL had improved, repaired by weight interpolation and a guardrail rule; Gemma-4-31B as a data teacher failing for 21 of 23 weak languages; the coverage limits of NLLB; and the limits of every benchmark we used.
9. *An audit of how the evaluation sets entered our pipeline* (Section 6.9a). A contamination audit (2026-10-26) traced the test sets through the SFT text, the RL prompts, the reward calibration and the selection of checkpoints. It found that the eight benchmarks rest on five English texts; that the SFT text held the English side of 95.3% of the FLORES+ devtest sentences and the reference translation itself for seven languages; that the Efik reward thresholds of RL-final were fitted on 997 of the 1,012 IbomMT test sentences and the final run's anchors on six test splits; that selection used development sets only; and that a cross-paper comparison on `clean` rows in earlier drafts was not like for like (removed). The conclusions that survive are stated in the section; the stage-by-stage account and the tables of exposure are generated from the audit's files.

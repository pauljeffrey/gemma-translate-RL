## 11 Limitations

1. **Exposure of the evaluation sets to our pipeline (contamination).** Section 6.9a gives the audit. The SFT text contains the English side of 95.3% of the FLORES+ devtest sentences (98.7% of dev), 91.2% of SmolSent, 84.4% of SALT, 39.9% of NTREX-128, 21.9% of MAFAND-MT and 7.2% of GlotStoryBook, and the reference translation itself for seven FLORES+ languages (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, Zulu, Tswana, Oromo, Luganda; 933 of the 1,012 sentences in at least one), eight MAFAND-MT languages (Igbo 90%, Yoruba 48%), eight SmolSent languages (Wolof 73%, Bambara 65%, Ewe 61%) and SALT Luganda (84%). We could not trace most of the source repositories because the SFT windows were not kept; one carrier is named (`African-Languages-Lab/multi-open` as repackaged in `<HF_ORG_LEGACY>/sft-new`) and the rest is UNKNOWN. The `no_pair` subset removes the reference-pair leak but not the English-side exposure; only `clean` (48 FLORES+ sentences per language) removes both, and it is small and harder, so levels on `all` and `no_pair` rows are inflated (FLORES+ about 3.8 chrF++ for SFT-final and 2.6 for RL-final, SmolSent 6.4 and 4.6) and we rely on differences between models that share the exposure and on `clean` rows. The eight test sets rest on five English texts. **The reward's calibration also used test splits:** the Efik thresholds of RL-final and of the final run (997 of the 1,012 IbomMT test sentences), the final run's anchors (test splits of NTREX-128, SmolSent, SALT, GlotStoryBook, IbomMT and FLORES-MOZ), its long-document limits (SmolDoc and AfriLongDoc core), the choice of its five NLLB-signal languages (FLORES+ devtest gaps), and the science stage's replay hold-out omitted devtest; the effect of these on the scores was not measured (no re-run exists), so the Efik IbomMT result of RL-final and the final run's results on those sets are not independent tests. The RL prompt pools were audited against the English side of the test sets (Section 6.9): exact overlap is at most 0.7% of the sentences of any benchmark (0.69% for FLORES+; at most 0.16% of the rows in the pool of the row's own language; 0 of the 2,160 FLORES+ `clean` rows), and RL prompts carry no target text; paraphrase and reward-model training data were not audited. We did not check whether the published systems saw FLORES+ (NLLB-200 used FLORES as its development set, for the others we do not know), so cross-paper comparisons carry an unknown bias, and our own exposure inflates our FLORES+ level by about 3 chrF++ (SFT) and 2 (RL), so any comparison on `all` or `no_pair` rows favours our model by about that amount relative to a system without exposure; we make no claim of superiority or inferiority over the Afrique LLMs.
2. **Religious-text-heavy SFT data.** Jehovah's Witnesses-style text makes up about 58% of the replay tokens (62% with Bible-style text) (75-93% for most weak languages) and its publisher's copyright is not cleared. Models trained on it write in that register and our calibration of anchors for Esan, Edo, Ghomala, Okpe, Urhobo and others rests on it.
3. **An LLM-generated silver set.** The only check we have for Urhobo is 230 sentence pairs generated by Gemini and not verified by a native speaker; it was used as a check, never as a threshold, and our agreement with it (24.7 chrF++) is not a quality measure. Nineteen languages have no human-reference benchmark at all.
4. **Small development sets and interval widths.** Monitoring and selection used a 760-sentence dev set (FLORES+ dev and MAFAND dev rows, 40 per language, no devtest; minimum detectable change about 1.5 chrF++ for the macro; RL-final is the last step 1,433, not the dev-best step 986), weak-language monitors of 60 sentences per language (about 3 per language), 25 development papers per language for paper-level quality, and long-document intervals that resample 35-100 documents; our bootstrap intervals cover sentence noise, not language sampling or RL seeds. **Every RL configuration was run once**; we have no seed variance.
5. **QE metric reliability.** AfriCOMET-QE and SSA-COMET were validated mostly against human scores for *other* systems; they are weak rankers within a group; twi and xho are near chance for all metrics; the 23 consensus-only languages have no validated metric; the calibration anchors of 21 of 41 languages have a band of only 0.05 QE units between the failure line and the human median.
6. **No human evaluation of our outputs.** All quality statements are chrF++, spBLEU, AfriCOMET, SSA-COMET, LLM-judge or reward-model scores. chrF++ rewards surface overlap and the consensus reward is built from it. The pairwise comparison of Section 6.9 is a stand-in, not a replacement: it counts how often metrics prefer the RL sentence, the metrics agree on a sentence's winner in only 64-73% of the cases, AfriCOMET with reference is not independent of the reward, and the learned metrics were validated on a subset of our languages. An independent LLM judge (Gemini-3.8-Flash, SSA-COMET prompts) was validated against human ratings of other systems' output (300 segments, eight language pairs, pooled Spearman 0.49-0.51; Sections 7.1a and 6.12) and applied to our models' output; it is a model, not a human, it tracks human ratings only at about 0.5 (so we read only language-level, pooled or very large differences), and it is not a substitute for native-speaker evaluation, which has still not been done.
7. **Long-document claims are narrow.** Our models generate the whole document natively; the sentence-by-sentence mode is a wrapper used to compare with systems that cannot take long input. Native whole-document translation works only for the science-stage checkpoints and only up to about 2,048 tokens, omits 13-30% of the sentences, and is below the sentence-by-sentence output of NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM (Section 6.10); the science-stage models and the final run were trained on the AfriScience training split used by two of the evaluation sets; stitched documents test length, not discourse; no human judge of discourse was used, and the LLM judge of Section 6.12 scored aligned units of one to a few sentences, not discourse (it scores the final checkpoint's whole-document units at 25.8 of 100 against 67.0 for its wrapper, five languages; chrF++ against pseudo-references is much less harsh than this judge, and the whole-document failure is not specific to the Ge'ez-script languages); several systems were run on fewer languages. **Pseudo-reference bias.** Long-document references from NLLB (and MADLAD) are sentence-by-sentence translations that favour chunked hypotheses by construction and (NLLB) score the model 1-16 chrF++ above human references; human long documents beyond 680 tokens are stitched from unrelated documents, so discourse quality is not measured.
8. **Coverage of the reference systems and of the metrics.** NLLB-200 covers 44 of the 77 languages and none of the consensus-only languages but one; for 33 languages no published per-language number from any system we compared exists. Published numbers are used as reported (FLORES-200, different decoding, metric implementations not documented for some tables). AfriCOMET is published for almost none of the baselines and scores only 42 of our 45 FLORES+ languages (54 of 77 overall), so the like-for-like comparison of Section 6.8 rests on our own re-scoring of the systems we could run (NLLB-200 600M and 3.3B, TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B, 2B for 9 languages): the other 4-14B models appear only with published chrF++ and SSA-COMET, on different sentences and prompts; NLLB is decoded with beam 4 and ours greedily; the intervals contain no language-sampling noise; and our coverage statements (Section 6.8) rest on manual tallies of other papers' language tables that we could not verify.
9. **Causes of the 2b divergence and of the plateau are not isolated;** three changes were made together in 2c.
10. **The final run is not one clean trajectory, and its evaluation is narrower than that of RL-final.** It ended at step 401 (wound down after step 350, Section 6.7), but it had a restart at step 150 (KL guard reset, and apparently a change of the padding detector, so that training and evaluation rewards are not comparable across it) and two OOM kills with a resume from the step-250 checkpoint with a changed learning-rate scale; its stop criteria are quoted from the project lead's instruction, not from a file; all numbers of Section 6.7 are development sets without confidence intervals (the paper-level series moved by 0.63 chrF++ between two evaluations one optimiser step apart); only the last step was kept (the best evaluation reward, at step 175, is not available as weights). The final checkpoint (private Hub repository, branch `latest`) was scored after the run on FLORES+ devtest, NTREX-128, MAFAND-MT, SmolSent and AfriScience-MT dev and test (Section 6.7): it is level with RL-final on the general benchmarks (+0.2 chrF++, -0.002 to -0.005 AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference, preferred in 52% of the non-tied sentences by chrF++ and in 47-48% by those two metrics; one run, intervals over sentences only; AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET are not held-out for this run because its reward contained a QE ensemble of their reference-free versions, and its anchors and long-document limits were fitted on the test splits of NTREX-128, SmolSent, SALT, GlotStoryBook, IbomMT, FLORES-MOZ, SmolDoc and AfriLongDoc core, so its benchmark results on those sets are not independent) and level with the science-stage start A2W on AfriScience-MT test at paper level; it was also run through the long-context benchmark of Section 6.10 (same chrF++ as the step-250 checkpoint, a higher loop rate in whole-document mode), after the other systems and with one run per checkpoint. Learned metrics were not computed on the AfriScience documents (too long for their encoders), the pairwise learned-metric comparison for SSA-COMET with reference rests on the hash sample of the earlier comparison (22 languages), and its AfriScience dev scores are not independent of its checkpoint-selection rule (the checkpoint itself is simply the last step). The headline model of the paper is RL-final of phase 2c (a recommendation to the owner is in `paper/TODO.md`; TODO(owner: decide the headline model)). **The controlled ablation of Section 8.4 has finished, and it is a short, four-language, one-seed proxy** (not the production multi-phase RL: 49 steps and 50,176 completions per arm, 2.9% of the completions of the main run), its weak start is a re-created, annealed short SFT with 12.7% of the SFT tokens and not a snapshot of the real run, its continued-SFT controls use our own commercially clean data with one untuned recipe, there is no seed-to-seed variance in any interval, it contains no human evaluation, and part of the weak-start chrF++ gain is repair of unfinished outputs (on sentences finished by every system the weak start gains less in chrF++ than the strong start); the research question of Section 8 is therefore not answered. **The widened pseudo-reference reward of Section 7.8 has not been trained on.**
11. **Licence constraints.** The weights are bound by the Gemma Terms of Use; the licence audit of the SFT corpus records unstated-licence sources (213 of 292 scanned repositories), Jehovah's Witnesses text with uncleared publisher copyright and two owner-approved non-commercial exceptions, while the project lead reports a verification that found no non-commercial data, which is not in the repository (TODO(owner: attach the licence verification)); the NLLB (CC-BY-NC) pseudo-references enter the final run as a reward signal for five languages (Section 12).
12. **CPU numbers come from a shared server, and the laptop speed is an estimate.** The speed of Section 6.11 was measured on four pinned cores of a shared Xeon Gold 6126 while other jobs ran on the machine (7-17% slowdown for runs measured in parallel; the headline rows are solo runs), with 120-sentence timing sets, three repetitions at most (one for some rows), six languages (Tigrinya in place of Nigerian Pidgin, which FLORES+ lacks) and the final checkpoint rather than the headline model; the laptop figures (1-2 sentences per second, 0.5-1.0 times the measured speed) are an estimate that we did not measure, and the explanation of why the 270M model is not faster than NLLB-200-distilled-600M greedy is a hypothesis. The tokenisation-cost finding (Section 3.3) is measured on CPU only, its association with chrF++ is correlational, and the restricted-vocabulary mitigation is untested (TODO(vocab)). NLLB-200 appears in Section 6.11 as a reference with a different decoder, without intervals.
13. **Output is not safe for high-stakes use.** The weak language groups score about 15-27 chrF++ on FLORES+; science text of health and agriculture is among the training domains; no safety or toxicity evaluation was done.
14. **The extra reward hardening of the final run is not shown to protect against anything, and has no control run.** The anchors, the QE ensemble and the long-document reward v2 are a design with measured mechanisms (Sections 4.3, 4.4, 7.2-7.4); the stored-output check of Section 7.9 finds that RL-final did not show the failures the copy terms target, that the final checkpoint is level with it on most indicators (names lost +1.6 pp), that padding and repetition are 2-4 pp lower in the weak and consensus-only languages (mostly on one benchmark) and that whole-document output is far better but mostly through the science stage. No run from the same start with the plain reward exists, so none of these differences can be attributed to the extra terms; the benchmark outputs are greedy while training used T=1 samples; and the final run and RL-final differ in start model, data, prompts and schedule.
15. **The LLM-judge results are indicative, not definitive.** (a) The judge is validated at Spearman about 0.5 on 300 segments of other systems' output (25-50 per language pair; Somali and Xhosa not usable for the reference-free prompt, Kinyarwanda and Igbo only just usable), so single sentences and the 2-7 point language-level gaps of Run 2 carry little weight; only the pooled Run 2 difference (+4.04 [3.21, 4.86]) and the 20-80 point gaps of Run 3 are well above that noise. (b) The reference-free prompt was validated on sentences; Run 3 judges blocks of about 1.4 sentences for the whole-document systems and single sentences for the wrapper and baselines, and the alignment of a drifting output is uncertain (2-5% of whole-document units matched a shifted block better; larger drifts are not checked). (c) The judge is a reference-free LLM and harsher than chrF++ against pseudo-references; we did not test whether it prefers NLLB-like text. (d) Run 2 uses the `no_pair` rows only (Hausa 170 sentences) and both systems saw the English side of about 95% of the sentences; on the 129 sampled `clean` sentences the pooled difference is +3.4 [0.3, 6.2] against +4.0 [3.2, 4.9] on all sampled sentences (per language n = 5 to 48 and no interval: positive in six of eight, negative in Xhosa and Somali), and the validation material overlaps our training and evaluation text (the SSA-MTE train split is in the SFT text; the English of AfriMTE-WMT2024 is 100% FLORES+ devtest; Section 6.12, caveat 7). (e) One run of a hosted model that may change; no human reference for Run 3; whole-document modes of NLLB and TranslatePsy were not judged. (f) Existing NLLB-200 3.3B and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B outputs were sent to the API for evaluation only; no TranslatePsy data was used in any experiment. (g) The judge is not the one of the SSA-COMET paper (Gemini-2.5-Pro was not available to our key); we chose Gemini-3.8-Flash over Gemini-3.1-Pro-preview on 300 segments and do not know how it compares with 2.5 Pro.
16. **The Ge'ez-script gap is described, not explained.** Amharic and Tigrinya are the languages in which the model is furthest behind NLLB (Section 6.8: the gap to NLLB-200 3.3B is 3.85 chrF++ over 44 languages and 3.32 over the 42 without them, which is the headline comparison; Amharic 19.0 and Tigrinya 11.0 alone) and Amharic dominates the wrapper's gap in the judge comparison (Section 6.12). Leaving the two languages out of the headline is a choice about presentation because they are an outlier; it does not mean the gap is understood or unimportant. The mean gap to NLLB is skewed by a few languages (median 3.17 over all 44 and 2.78 over the 42; Kabuverdianu 21.0, Amharic 19.0 and Tigrinya 11.0 carry 30% of the summed gap), and Kabuverdianu, a creole that is also our largest gain over SFT (+14.2), is kept in the headline (Section 6.8). We did not test any of four candidate causes: (a) capacity sharing of 270M parameters across 77 languages; (b) tokenisation (only 862 Ethiopic entries in the 262,144-token Gemma vocabulary; 3.03 and 3.46 tokens per word for Amharic and Tigrinya against 2.09 for Swahili); (c) the amount and quality of the Amharic and Tigrinya training data; (d) Gemma's pretraining coverage of the two languages. NLLB is larger, trained on far more data and on many more language pairs, so the comparison with it cannot separate them, and the whole-document failure does not depend on the Ge'ez languages. Future work: a matched specialist-model and larger-model experiment; no such result is reported here.
17. **A fresh evaluation is planned, not done.** Nothing in this paper is measured on text that our pipeline has never seen, apart from the `clean` rows, which are few and harder. The audit surveyed the options (2026-10-26): the WMT22 African test material is FLORES-based; WMT24++ has only Swahili (two locales) and Zulu (998 segments each, references are human post-edits of machine translation); WMT25 has only English to Maasai; AfriMTE-WMT2024 uses FLORES+ devtest sentences as its English; NTREX-128 is WMT19 newstest and already used. The recommended set is three layers: (1) the SSA-MTE test split (news, 204-245 English sentences for each of amh, hau, kik, kin, luo, twi, yor; its train split is in our SFT text and 2 windows of the SFT sample carry English of its test split, so its exposure is not zero and not measured); (2) WMT24++ English to Swahili and Zulu; (3) a newly commissioned human-translated set of 300-500 English sentences per language, written after the data cutoff of the next model, for languages that no public set covers (for example Kabuverdianu, Kabiye, Nuer, Kamba, Bambara), translated by two independent paid translators and a reviewer and sealed until all decisions are logged. **The cost estimate of the audit, 7,000 to 18,000 USD for ten languages (88,000 words at an assumed 0.08-0.20 USD per word, 3-6 weeks), has no source behind it** and is a guess about market prices; a pilot with two or three languages is cheaper. No result on any fresh set exists, and results on one are future work.

## 3 Languages, data and the supervised model

### 3.1 Task, model and output format

The task is English-to-African-language translation with one fixed prompt, `Translate the text to {language}:  {english}` (two spaces after the colon), and one fixed answer format, `{iso} {translation}<end_of_turn>`: the model first writes the three-letter tag of the target language and then the translation. The tag is part of the loss in SFT and is *required* by the RL reward (a missing or wrong tag is a hard failure), so that the model cannot silently answer in another language. Only English sources are used. The model is `google/gemma-3-270m-it` (18 layers, hidden size 640, 4 attention heads with one key-value head, sliding window 512, 262,144-token vocabulary; the embedding table alone holds about 168 M of the roughly 270 M parameters by our count, about 170 M according to the model announcement) [@gemmateam2025gemma3; @google2025gemma270m]. We train the full model; no adapters.

### 3.2 Languages and groups

The language table has 85 rows, of which 77 are the *target languages of SFT and of the RL phases* and 8 are table entries that were not RL-trained (`nde`, `fat`, `knc`, `gaa`, `men`, `luy`, `tzm`, `ful`); according to `gemma_mt/languages.py` and D-38 none of these 8 has SFT training data, so they are zero-shot (one report lists four of them as having some data; TODO(owner: confirm)), and only Kanuri and North Ndebele are scored on a benchmark. The 77 RL languages are split by the *content signal* that is available to the reward ({{tref:t_groups_def}}): 42 languages whose AfriCOMET-QE score was validated against human references (AUC for human versus mismatched references of at least 0.70), 12 languages named on the AfriCOMET card or varieties of a validated language but without reference-based validation (QE is blended 50/50 with a consensus signal), and 23 languages that AfriCOMET does not cover, for which the reward has no learned metric at all (consensus signal plus constraint penalties). Of the 85 rows, 66 have at least one human-reference benchmark in our evaluation (45 on FLORES+); 19 have none, 13 of them RL languages ({{tref:t_nobench}}, {{fref:fig8_coverage}}). For these 13 languages quality was judged only by the reward models.

### 3.3 Tokenizer

We keep the Gemma 3 tokenizer unchanged. The relevant measured facts are modest: the number of Gemma tokens of the human reference per Gemma token of the English source ranges from 1.12 to 3.19 (median 1.88) over the 54 targets with human documents (`reports/longdoc_benchmark_plan_2026-10-20_parts/data/fertility.json`, `DECISIONS.md` D-44), so a 2,048-token English document translates into an output of roughly 4,000 tokens or more in the more fragmented languages; the tokenizer has no normaliser, and decomposed (NFD) text of a diacritic-rich language costs up to 1.29 times as many tokens as NFC text, so all training text, rewards and metrics are Unicode-NFC (`DECISIONS.md` D-29; memory notes on the NFC audit). Gemma token counts are 0.88-0.90 times the AfroXLM-R token counts for the English sources and 0.97-1.43 times for the human references (54 languages; same file), and the AfroXLM-R counts define our length bins.

**Tokenisation cost by language (a measured finding).** The same sentence costs a very different number of tokens in different languages, and a decoder pays for every output token. On the 1,012 FLORES+ devtest sentences the Gemma tokenizer gives 3.11 characters per token for Swahili, 2.99 for Hausa, 2.74 for Kinyarwanda, 1.94 for Yoruba, 1.66 for Amharic and 1.35 for Tigrinya (English: 4.87; `reports/data/weak_language_data_2026-10-22/tokenizer_flores_devtest.csv`, {{tref:t_tokcost_lang}}, {{fref:fig17_token_cost}}). On the 170-200 sentences per language of the CPU speed set (Section 6.11) the order is the same (2.92, 2.88, 2.65, 1.87, 1.61 and 1.31 characters per reference token, derived from `tokens_per_sentence.csv`). The English source is equally long in all six runs (26.6-28.5 Gemma tokens), so the difference is entirely on the output side: the reference translation into Yoruba or Tigrinya has 1.50-1.51 times as many tokens as the Swahili one, and our model writes 76.3 and 74.3 tokens per sentence against 44.5 for Swahili (1.71 and 1.67 times). Characters per token is not the whole story: Amharic has the second-lowest characters per token but Amharic sentences are short (88 reference characters against 136 for Swahili), so it costs only 1.24 times the Swahili output tokens. On four CPU cores with the Q8\_0 build the consequence is direct: Yoruba and Tigrinya run at 1.58 and 1.55 sentences/s against 2.46 for Swahili at batch 32, that is 1.56 and 1.59 times as long per sentence (about 176-179 hours per million sentences against 113 for Swahili and 111 for Hausa; best of two repetitions; one Kinyarwanda repetition ran at 0.42 sentences/s against 2.11 for the other, probably because of another job on the shared machine, which is why the best repetition is used). The "about 1.6 times" in a casual statement of this finding is therefore the time per sentence; the output-token ratio is 1.7 for the generated text and 1.5 for the references. Time and memory of a deployment thus depend on the target language, and a throughput figure without the language (such as the 139 hours per million sentences of Section 6.11, a mixture of six languages) hides a factor of about 1.6.

The languages that cost most per sentence are also those the model translates worst: in the six languages the order by characters per token and by chrF++ of the final-run checkpoint is identical (58.0, 45.7, 43.5, 29.4, 19.3, 13.8 from Swahili to Tigrinya), and over the 16 languages with both numbers the Spearman correlation of characters per token with the chrF++ of RL-final is 0.91 ({{fref:fig17_token_cost}}a). We read this as an association, not a mechanism: fragmentation of a language by the vocabulary goes with little pretraining text, little SFT text (Section 3.4; in the weak-language report one million Amharic tokens are about 330 thousand words against about 540 thousand for Hausa), script and morphology, and Oromo (2.71 characters per token, 31.3 chrF++) and Bambara (2.24, 24.9) lie off the line. Two implications follow. First, the cost of a deployment is not uniform: a service that translates into Tigrinya or Yoruba needs about 1.6 times the compute per sentence of one that translates into Swahili. Second, the output budget must be set per language, because the same English text needs 1.5 to 2.6 times (Section 9.1) as many output tokens in the fragmenting languages, which also lengthens the context and the cache of long documents (not measured). We measured the cost on CPU only; on a GPU the number of decoding steps scales with output tokens in the same way, but we did not measure it.

*A possible mitigation, untested.* TODO(vocab: idea only, no experiment has been run and no number of this paper depends on it; a follow-up will add the result here). Since a decoder pays for a softmax over the whole vocabulary at every step, and 168M of the 270M parameters are that 262,145-entry table (which is also the output head), one could restrict decoding to a per-language subset of the vocabulary: the tokens that occur in the target-language training text (for example those covering 99.9% of the target-language token occurrences of the SFT corpus) plus the language tag, digits, punctuation, a Latin core for names and loanwords, and the special tokens. This would cut the cost of each output token (and, for a single-language deployment, the size of the table held in memory), but not the number of tokens per sentence, which is a property of the tokenizer: Yoruba and Tigrinya would stay about 1.5 to 1.7 times Swahili in tokens. Open questions: how much chrF++ is lost on names, numerals and code-switched text; whether a model trained with the full softmax tolerates the renormalisation without fine-tuning; how large the speed-up is when the rest of the step (attention, feed-forward, memory traffic) is unchanged; and whether the per-token cost is dominated by the output projection at all (Section 6.11 states this as a hypothesis).

### 3.4 Supervised fine-tuning data

All parallel data are *existing* public datasets (we did not create translations); they were regrouped, not newly translated. The sources include benchmark-derived data (found by the audit of Section 6.9a): the SSA-MTE train split (English source with the human reference), SMOL translations (Bambara, Wolof, Ewe), MAFAND-MT test pairs inside aggregated collections, and FLORES+ rows inside aggregated Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa, Luganda and Zulu collections (`African-Languages-Lab/multi-open`, repackaged in `<HF_ORG_LEGACY>/sft-new`, holds 23 FLORES+ devtest and 36 dev sentences with their Igbo translation; the other carriers could not be traced because the SFT windows were not backed up). 82% of the original pairs are single sentences, so consecutive pairs of the same source and language pair were concatenated into *windows* (pseudo-paragraphs, no newline) in four length bins by the English side in AfroXLM-R tokens (b64, b256, b512, b1024; token-balanced, 20-29% of the tokens per bin), so that the model sees long inputs, order and sentence count during SFT; discourse in the true sense comes only from the RL documents. Windows are packed into 4,096-token packs, one language per pack, with block-diagonal attention so that packed samples never see each other and positions restart per sample. The final build has 11,442,172 windows, 538,480 training packs and 2.204 B tokens (1.318 B target tokens), 77 target languages, forward direction (English to X) only. Pairs were vetted with AfriCOMET-QE where the language is covered (bottom 15% per language and QE below 0.20 removed) and with rule-based checks otherwise; training text is NFC (`DECISIONS.md` D-14 and the memory notes; `reports/gemma_sft_training_plan_2026-10-14.md`).

Two properties of this corpus matter for the interpretation of every later result. First, it is dominated by religious text for the low-resource languages: in the replay pool sampled from the SFT windows (87,498 windows, 77 languages) Jehovah's Witnesses / Bible-style text makes up on average about 58% of the tokens and 75-93% for most of the weakest languages (`reports/weak_language_data_2026-10-22.md` section 0, item 4). Second, a large part of it comes from repositories whose licence is unstated: of 292 scanned repositories 213 had no stated licence and were used with a flag, about 58% of the replay tokens are Jehovah's Witnesses text whose publisher copyright is not cleared, and the audit records two owner-approved non-commercial exceptions (`reports/weak_language_data_2026-10-22.md` section 5). The project lead reports a separate verification that found no non-commercial data in the SFT mixture; that verification is not in the repository (TODO(owner: attach the licence verification)) (Section 11).

### 3.5 SFT optimisation

SFT ran in two stages with one set of weights (`reports/gemma_sft_training_plan_2026-10-14.md`; `gemma_mt/configs/sft_unimax.yaml`). Stage A: AdamW, learning rate 5e-5 with 3% warm-up and cosine decay, betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.01, gradient clip 1.0, fp32 master weights with bf16 autocast, 131,072 tokens per step (32 packs), languages drawn with probability proportional to the square root of their number of packs (floor 0.2%, a language is retired after four passes over its data), stopped at about step 4,750. Stage B (restart with a fresh optimiser and schedule): Muon with learning rate 4e-5 matched to the AdamW update scale, a UniMax-style language budget of 3 B tokens in which every pack of every language is used between one and two times and 20 priority languages get four times the weight. The final checkpoint is step 27,638 of the combined run, with a macro validation loss (mean of the 77 per-language losses) of 1.2654. All languages use the same weights; the choice among checkpoints was made by macro validation loss, which was still falling slowly (1.2706 at step 25,750 to 1.2654 at step 27,638). A checkpoint of this run at step 20,500 (macro validation loss 1.3025) was benchmarked against the untouched base model: on the FLORES+ rows whose English sentence is not in the SFT text the SFT model reaches 27.0 chrF++ against 13.0 for `gemma-3-270m-it` (`reports/gemma_sft_benchmarks_2026-10-18.md` section 2.1), so the base model contributes little African-language ability by itself.

### 3.6 Evaluation data, metrics and the contamination split

We evaluate English-to-X on eight human-reference benchmarks, always on *test* splits: FLORES+ devtest (1,012 sentences, 45 of our languages) [@oldi2025floresplus; @goyal2022flores101; @nllb2022nllb], NTREX-128 (1,997 sentences, 24), MAFAND-MT test (15 languages) [@adelani2022mafand], SmolSent (863 sentences, 45) [@caswell2025smol], IbomMT (Ibibio, Efik), FLORES-MOZ (Sena), SALT (5) [@akera2022salt] and GlotStoryBook (8). **These eight test sets rest on five independent English texts:** FLORES+ devtest, IbomMT and FLORES-MOZ share the same 1,012 English sentences, and the others are NTREX-128, MAFAND-MT, SmolSent (with SmolDoc) and SALT with GlotStoryBook. Metrics are chrF++ (sacreBLEU, word order 2) [@popovic2017chrfpp] and spBLEU (flores200 tokenizer), corpus-level per language and subset, macro-averaged over languages, on NFC text. Greedy decoding with up to 768 new tokens unless stated; a completion that does not end inside the budget is scored as it stands, and the *finished rate* is reported next to it. No test split was used as a training target. Test splits were used as *calibration or selection* material in four places, listed in Section 6.9a: the Efik reward (IbomMT test), the anchors and long-document limits of the final run (NTREX-128, SmolSent, SALT, GlotStoryBook, IbomMT, FLORES-MOZ, SmolDoc, AfriLongDoc core), the choice of the five NLLB-signal languages of the final run (FLORES+ devtest) and the decoding study (FLORES+ devtest).

The SFT text contains the English side of 95.3% of the FLORES+ devtest sentences (and 98.7% of dev), 91.2% of SmolSent, 84.4% of SALT, 39.9% of NTREX-128, 21.9% of MAFAND-MT and 7.2% of GlotStoryBook, and for some languages the reference translation itself (FLORES+ devtest: Hausa 83%, Igbo 55%, Yoruba 35% of the sentences, 933 of 1,012 sentences in at least one language; MAFAND-MT test: Igbo 90%, Yoruba 48%; SALT: Luganda 84%; SmolSent: Wolof 73%, Bambara 65%, Ewe 61%). We could not trace most of the source repositories because the SFT windows were not kept; an 87,498-window sample of the windows shows that the FLORES+ English entered through single-sentence Luganda windows with an independent Luganda translation and through Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa and Zulu windows, some of which carry the FLORES+ translation (Section 6.9a). Every number is therefore reported on three subsets: `all` (every row), `no_pair` (rows whose reference translation was not in the SFT text; 96% of the FLORES+ rows; the headline) and `clean` (rows whose English sentence was never seen; only 48 FLORES+ sentences per language, so per-language `clean` figures are noisy). **`no_pair` removes the reference-pair leak but not the English-side exposure; only `clean` removes both.** Levels on `all` and `no_pair` rows are inflated: on FLORES+ the SFT-final macro is 28.9 on all rows and 25.1 on `clean` rows (RL-final 34.3 and 31.7); we therefore use differences between models that share the exposure, and `clean` rows, as evidence, not levels. Confidence intervals are 95% paired bootstrap intervals over sentences (1,000 resamples; 500 for the per-language table) of the SFT-to-RL difference; they measure sentence-sampling noise, not language-sampling noise, because every language is weighted equally and the language set is fixed. The RL prompt pools were audited against the English side of the test sets for this paper: exact overlap is at most 0.7% of the sentences of any benchmark (Section 6.9). The audit covers the prompts; the reward's thresholds were fitted on human references (FLORES+ dev and MAFAND dev; for Efik the IbomMT test set), see Section 6.9a.

## 14 Conclusion

Reinforcement learning with a quality-estimation reward, shaped on human anchors and surrounded by hard failure classes, improved a supervised 270M-parameter translator for 77 African languages on every human-reference benchmark we tried (+5.4 chrF++ on FLORES+, 45 of 45 languages up, and +6.6 on the rows whose English was never in our training text, with a positive gain on the never-seen rows of all eight benchmarks, although the levels of both models are inflated by the exposure of the test sets to our SFT text and, for Efik and for the final run, to the reward calibration, Section 6.9a; sentence by sentence the RL output is preferred by chrF++ in 63.7% and by reference-based AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET in about 70% of the sentences on which the models differ, which is metric agreement with human references and not a human evaluation; an independent LLM judge validated at Spearman about 0.5 against human ratings also prefers it, +4.0 [3.2, 4.9] on a 0-100 scale over 1,570 FLORES+ sentences in eight languages), most for the languages where the supervised model was weakest and where the reward had no learned metric (Kabuverdianu +14.2, Kabiye +14.1, Kamba +12.1 chrF++, part of it repaired unfinished output), while leaving it 3.3 chrF++ behind a model twelve times its size (on identical sentences over the 42 languages without Amharic and Tigrinya: 3.32 chrF++ and 0.043 AfriCOMET behind NLLB-200 3.3B and 1.13 and 0.028 behind NLLB-200-distilled-600M; over all 44 languages 3.85 and 0.050, and 1.69 and 0.035; the median chrF++ gap is 2.78 and 3.17 because a few languages carry the mean, Kabuverdianu (21.0, also our largest gain over SFT, +14.2) first; the two Ge'ez-script languages, an outlier whose cause we have not tested, are 19.0 and 13.8 chrF++ against 38.0 and 24.7 for the 3.3B model: we do not beat NLLB, and the gap is 3.16 on the never-seen rows of the identical sentences); we make no claim of superiority or inferiority over the published 4-14B LLMs. Its distinguishing property is breadth at small scale: 77 African target languages in 270M parameters, the widest African coverage among open models below 1B parameters that we found (55 for NLLB-200-600M), which is a statement about coverage and not about quality or priority. The reward carries most of the engineering: per-language shaping on human anchors, language-aware alignment so that omissions cost something, a consensus signal for languages without a metric and hard failure classes in the headline run, and in the final run an ensemble that takes the pessimistic judge when two metrics disagree, a set of constraint detectors whose false-positive rates we measured against human text, and a long-document reward. We call the final run's reward hardened as a description of that design. We did not find that the extra terms protected against anything: RL-final did not copy the source or stuff its outputs in training, the final checkpoint is level with it on most indicators (names lost are higher), padding and repetition are 2-4 pp lower in the weak and consensus-only languages and whole-document output is far better, but there is no control run with the plain reward from the same start, and most of the whole-document improvement was already in the science-stage model (Section 7.9). Training was unstable once (phase 2b) and the sentence-level gain was complete after roughly the first million completions. The length curriculum did less than we hoped: RL-final translates documents of 2,048 and 4,096 tokens only when decoding is chunked by sentence, and its single-pass translation works to about 512 tokens. After the supervised science stage the model finishes whole documents of up to about 2,048 tokens natively (chrF++ 39.4-39.6 and 28.2-28.8 against human references at about 1,000 and 2,000 tokens, where NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM score 5.9-14.9 in whole-document mode), but it omits 13-30% of the sentences, and with a sentence-by-sentence wrapper (our models generate the whole document natively; the wrapper is how we compare with systems that cannot take long input) every system, ours included, does better and NLLB and TranslatePsy are 1-4 chrF++ ahead: better on native long input is not better document translation, and we do not claim to translate documents better than other systems; an independent LLM judge agrees, scoring the final checkpoint's aligned whole-document units at 25.8 of 100 against 67.0 for its wrapper and 85.6 for NLLB-200 3.3B (five languages, validated only at Spearman about 0.5, Section 6.12). In practice the model can be run on a CPU with the GGUF Q8\_0 build, which matches the GPU output (-0.02 chrF++, [-0.22, +0.16]) at about two sentences per second on four server cores (laptop speed estimated at 1-2 sentences per second); the cost of a sentence depends on the language through the tokenizer (Yoruba and Tigrinya about 1.6 times Swahili), and the languages that cost most are those where the model is weakest. Whether RL can partly replace large-scale parallel SFT, as the Ge'ez languages suggest, is a question our data raise and do not answer. A small controlled ablation (four languages, 49 RL steps, one seed per arm, a pre-registered rule) is consistent with RL being a useful complement: RL from a weak SFT start (12.7% of the SFT tokens) gained +7.36 chrF++ [6.80, 7.85] against +5.72 [5.15, 6.30] from SFT-final, and beat continued SFT of equal GPU time by +7.04 [6.49, 7.54], but it ended 6.25 chrF++ [5.66, 6.87] below SFT-final, and on the sentences that every system finished its chrF++ gain was the smaller one (+1.13 against +2.23), so a large part of that gain repairs unfinished outputs; the data do not show that RL replaces SFT. The final science-and-general run (401 steps; interrupted by two memory kills and resumed from its step-250 checkpoint) moved the sentence-level development scores by at most +0.4 chrF++ and ended with 97% of the development papers finished in one pass against 93% at its start; we read it as a stable continuation of RL and not as a further gain. Its final checkpoint, scored afterwards, is level with RL-final on the general benchmarks (about +0.2 chrF++, but 0.001-0.005 lower AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference, preferred in 52% of the non-tied sentences by chrF++ and in 47-48% by the two learned metrics; one run), as good as the step-250 checkpoint on the long-input benchmark (Section 6.10) and, on the AfriScience-MT test split, better than RL-final by 2.4 sentence chrF++ and 13 paper-level chrF but not distinguishable from the science-stage model it started from at paper level; it is not the headline model of this paper.

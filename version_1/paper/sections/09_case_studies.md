## 9 Case studies

Three cases show where the recipe worked, where its signal was missing, and what a single piece of unverified evidence can and cannot do. Each case is deliberately narrow; the numbers come from the tables of Sections 6-8 and the reports named there.

### 9.1 Ge'ez script: Amharic and Tigrinya

*Why they may be hard for a 270M model (candidate causes; none was tested).* Amharic and Tigrinya are the only Ge'ez-script languages in our set. Their text is expensive for the tokenizer: on FLORES+ devtest the Gemma 3 vocabulary needs 1.9 times as many tokens for the Amharic translation as for the English source (2.5 times for Tigrinya), whereas the Qwen3 vocabulary needs 4.1 and 4.2 times and the gpt-oss vocabulary 5.8 and 6.0 times, and Yoruba and Zulu need 2.4 and 2.0 times for Gemma (1.5 to 2.6 times over the 17 Latin-script targets of the same file) ({{tref:t_tokcost}}; `docs/report/tokenizer_comparison_flores_plus.json`). A separate one-off probe with a wider set of small-model tokenizers, on a single 74-character Amharic passage and its English translation, gives 2.07 times for Gemma against 3.9 times for Qwen3-0.6B, 4.9 for BLOOM-560M, 6.9 for SmolLM2 and GPT-2 and 7.5 for Pythia-160M (`paper/data/tokenizer_amharic_probe_2026-10-23.csv`, `paper/scripts/tokenizer_amharic_probe.py`); since it is one short passage it supports the ranking only, and the FLORES+ numbers of {{tref:t_tokcost}} are the main evidence. Gemma is therefore comparatively kind to Ge'ez, yet Amharic and Tigrinya still cost about three times as many tokens per character as Latin-script languages (0.60-0.74 Gemma tokens per character against 0.21-0.36), only 862 of the 262,144 tokenizer vocabulary entries contain Ethiopic characters, and the SFT corpus holds only 8.29 M (Amharic) and 5.60 M (Tigrinya) target tokens. They were among the languages with the weakest SFT output: Tigrinya (fourth lowest of 45) 9.4 chrF++ on FLORES+ and Amharic (eighth lowest) 14.5, with 76-94% of the completions finishing across benchmarks.

*What RL did.* RL raised every Ge'ez benchmark by 3.4 to 4.5 chrF++ with intervals that exclude zero, and the finished rate to 100% ({{tref:t_geez}}).

*What more parallel data did.* For these two languages we built 11.18 M (Amharic) and 8.31 M (Tigrinya) tokens of commercially usable new parallel text (GoURMET CC0 news, FineTranslations real-language web text, OLDI-type sets), plus non-commercial and unknown-licence sets that were not merged (`reports/weak_language_data_2026-10-22.md` section 4). Mixture B_v2 gave Tigrinya 2.0 M extra tokens (about 13 times its replay share of the stage) and Amharic 1.5 M plus 0.37 M share-alike and 0.14 M code-switch tokens; the NLLB-3.3B teacher mixture C3 added only 0.28 M tokens to each of the two languages on top of A2W (4.47 M over 27 languages). The effect, in chrF++ on the weak-language dev set (60 sentences per language; noise about 3 for a single language) relative to the RL model: Tigrinya +0.14 for the mixture without extra Ge'ez data (A2W, $\alpha=0.7$), +0.70 and +0.74 for the mixture with the 2.0 M tokens (B_v2_ibom, $\alpha=0.5$ and 0.7); Amharic sentences +1.05 and paper level +2.24 for A2W (these include science text of its own); C3 against A2W +0.55 for Tigrinya and +0.02 for Amharic, +0.29 on average for the two ({{tref:t_sft_stage_c3}}; `reports/final_run2_integration_2026-10-22.md` section 1). Amharic paper-level quality *fell* with the larger Ge'ez mixture (B_v2_ibom: 20.51 for the RL model, 18.81 and 17.60 at $\alpha=0.5$ and 0.7, finished 92% to 80% and 68%). Against that RL gave +4.4 and +4.3 on the two FLORES+ sets.

*The final run.* The final run, which continues RL with the amended reward (401 steps, Section 6.7), moved Amharic by +0.31 on the AfriScience dev sentences (25.28 at the start, 25.65 at step 250, 25.59 at step 401; 200 sentences) and, at paper level, from 22.57 at the start to 25.33 at step 50, 24.71 at step 250 and 23.50 at step 401 (a noisy series of 25 documents; the finished rate of the Amharic papers is 0.92 at the start, 1.00 at steps 50-250 and 0.92 at step 401). The small Amharic gain at sentence level was therefore in place at step 250 and was not extended by the last 150 steps, and the paper-level gain of +0.94 at the end is smaller than at step 250 (+2.14) and of the size of the evaluation noise of that series (Section 6.7: 0.63 chrF++ macro between two evaluations one optimiser step apart). It is small against the +4.4 chrF++ that phases 1-2c gave the language on FLORES+ (14.5 to 18.9, Section 8.1), measured on a different benchmark and with a different model, so the two numbers are not additive.

*Reading.* This case supports statement (a) of Section 8: more parallel data, in the amounts we could add (a 24-36% increase of the existing target tokens of the language for the commercially usable Ge'ez additions of mixture B_v2), did not move Ge'ez quality by more than 0.7 chrF++ in the Ge'ez-specific comparisons (Tigrinya +0.70/+0.74 with B_v2_ibom, +0.55 with C3 against A2W, Amharic +0.02), while RL moved it by about 4; the larger numbers for A2W (+1.05 sentences, +2.24 paper level for Amharic) come from science and long-document text, not from added Ge'ez parallel data. It does not support the stronger claim, for the reasons of Section 8.2. The 19.0 (Amharic) and 11.0 (Tigrinya) point gap to NLLB-200 3.3B (37.9 and 24.8 chrF++ on FLORES-200 devtest) shows that the Ge'ez languages lack something that RL on this model did not provide. What that is, we did not test: Section 10 lists four candidate causes (capacity shared over 77 languages, tokenisation, the amount and quality of Amharic and Tigrinya data, Gemma's pretraining coverage), and the facts above (8 M and 5.6 M target tokens, a vocabulary with 862 Ethiopic entries) are consistent with several of them without singling one out.

### 9.2 Weak, consensus-only languages

*The group.* For 23 of the 77 languages (Esan, Edo, Ghomala, Okpe, Tswa, Dagbani, Duala, Basaa, Sidamo, Yao, Kabuverdianu, Krio and others) no learned metric exists and the content signal of the reward is the consensus among the samples of the group plus constraint penalties (Section 4.3). Their parallel text is overwhelmingly religious (75-93% of replay tokens for most of the weakest), only one of them (Kabuverdianu) is covered by NLLB-200, and 10 have no human-reference benchmark at all.

*What happened.* The consensus-only languages that do have a human-reference set improved at least as much as the validated ones: +7.69 [+7.30, +8.07] macro chrF++ on their primary benchmark for 13 languages against +4.97 for the 42 QE-validated, and +11.22 for the two on FLORES+ (Kabuverdianu and Makhuwa) (Section 6.1, {{tref:t_groups}}); most of that gain was in place at the end of the b64 stage of phase 2b (Section 6.3). The price is visible in the outputs: the share of outputs containing a five-gram found in at least three other prompts' outputs rose from 24.7% to 31.6% against 2.8% for human references, and the repetition and boilerplate flags of the detectors are highest exactly here (Esan 86% repetition, Edo 74%, Ghomala 66%; Sections 7.3 and 7.6). The consensus signal has an attractor in simulation, which the anchors close only partly, and the final run, which has the anchors, lowered the boilerplate rate of these languages by only 0.25 pp (Section 7.9): length-matched natural filler, a bag of words with the human function-word share and glosses padded with filler remain nearly undetected (Section 7.3).

*Can a stronger model supply data?* We asked whether Gemma-4-31B-it, used as a few-shot data teacher with eight retrieved examples per prompt, could help. It passed the pre-registered margin (+3 chrF++ with an interval above zero, plus quality checks) for only 2 of the 23 languages: Kabuverdianu (+16.1) and, conditionally, Krio (+3.5). It was not better for the other 21 (clearly worse for 17, level for Esan, Ghomala, South Ndebele and Herero), including all six named weak languages (Esan -1.1, Edo -5.9, Ghomala -2.4, Okpe -9.7, Tswa -3.3, Dagbani -10.3), and zero-shot it scored 1-14 chrF++ on 16 languages ({{tref:t_gemma31b}}). Its few-shot results look decent only because retrieval returns the religious phrases that our model has already absorbed (three examples instead of eight lose 6-12 points), and the held-out references are mostly religious text from our own corpus, which favours our model: the comparison is biased in our favour, and the out-of-domain rows are the fairer side. NLLB-200 does not cover 22 of the 23 languages (only Kabuverdianu), so its pseudo-references are used for five covered weak-signal languages only, of which Kabuverdianu is the one consensus-only language (Section 7.5).

*Reading.* For these languages the gains we measured are real against the human references we have, but they are also where the reward is least trustworthy and our evidence thinnest. We treat them as the clearest case of the limits of the approach: RL improved what the reward could see, a stronger teacher did not help, and without native-speaker evaluation we cannot say whether the outputs are better translations or only more consistent ones.

### 9.3 Urhobo: a silver set as a detector check

Urhobo (`urh`) illustrates a language with only religious text. In the first launch of the final run the padding detector flagged 26% of Urhobo completions as stuffed output (26-63% for Sidamo, Wolaytta, Basaa, Yao, Duala and Urhobo); the length bug of Section 7.3 explains the order of the failure rates of Sidamo, Wolaytta, Yao and two other languages, but not by itself those of Basaa, Duala and Urhobo. To check that the Urhobo detectors were not flagging human text we obtained 230 sentence pairs generated by Gemini (60 short phrases and 170 sentences), which were **not verified by a native speaker** and were therefore used as a check only: no threshold, function-word list, dictionary or weight was fitted on them (`reports/anchors_v3_2026-10-22.md` section 5). The check found 0 of 170 silver sentences flagged by the copy, stop-word and padding blocks (upper bound 2.2% at 95%), as for held-out religious text, so human-style Urhobo was not the problem; we could not reproduce the Urhobo failures without the live outputs. The set is also *not* like our corpus: 34.5% of its word types (14.4% of its tokens) are unseen in the Urhobo text of our SFT data, against 5.4% for held-out religious sentences, and it is templated (five connector types make up 21.6% of its tokens). Our models agree with it at 24.7 chrF++ (final general model; 22.9 for the start model of the final run), which is *not* a translation-quality measure and was never used to select a model. The lesson is methodological: a silver set can reveal a false-positive problem of a detector, but it cannot calibrate or validate a language; for Urhobo, as for the other languages without human references (Section 3.2), a native-speaker evaluation is the missing piece (Section 12).

## 2 Related work

**Machine translation for African languages.** The field's data and evaluation infrastructure comes from community efforts: the participatory Masakhane work [@nekoto2020participatory], MAFAND-MT, a news-domain corpus for 16 languages showing that a few thousand high-quality pairs go a long way when fine-tuning large pretrained models [@adelani2022mafand], AfroMT [@reid2021afromt], FLORES-200 and the NLLB-200 system covering 200 languages [@goyal2022flores101; @nllb2022nllb; @nllb2024nature], its community-maintained successor FLORES+ [@oldi2025floresplus; @dale2025oldi], MADLAD-400 [@kudugunta2023madlad], SALT for Ugandan languages [@akera2022salt], SMOL and GATITOS for very low-resource languages [@caswell2025smol], and the Bible and Jehovah's Witnesses corpora [@agic2019jw300; @akerman2023ebible] on which much low-resource training data still rests (an issue we measure in Section 3.4). Quality estimation for African languages is led by AfriCOMET [@wang2024africomet] and SSA-COMET, with human annotations for 14 pairs [@li2025ssacomet], the latter reported by the recent African-MT papers while AfriCOMET is published for almost none of the baseline systems (Section 6.8); AfroBench evaluates LLMs across 64 languages and finds a large gap to English [@ojo2025afrobench]. Recent LLM-based work trains much larger models than ours: AfriqueLLM adapts 4-14B Llama, Gemma and Qwen models with 16K context [@yu2026afriquellm], Sunflower post-trains Qwen3 14B/32B for 31 Ugandan languages [@akera2025sunflower], and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM is the nearest small-model competitor, 0.8B-4B models for 19 African languages trained by supervised fine-tuning with QE filtering and synthetic data [@gritta2026translatepsy]; smaller African-oriented models include InkubaLM (0.4B) [@tonja2024inkubalm] and a pruned NLLB-600M [@moslem2026afrinllb]. NLLB-200's own model card states that it was trained on inputs of at most 512 tokens and that it is not intended for document translation [@nllb200_card].

**Document-level and long-output translation.** LLMs can translate documents [@wang2023document]; open and specialised models are prone to off-target output [@wu2024adapting], translation quality of sequence models degrades on lengths unseen in training [@varis2021sequence], and long-context models use information in the middle of the input less reliably [@liu2024lost]; under-generation and repetition of long outputs are what we observe ourselves (Section 6.4). For African languages the dedicated resource is AfriDoc-MT, five languages and about 650 English words per document, where sentence-level models generalise poorly to long inputs and sentence-by-sentence translation beats pseudo-document translation for most models [@alabi2025afridocmt]; AfriScience-MT reports a large sentence-to-document quality drop on six languages [@abdulmumin2026afriscience]; AfriqueLLM reports document translation on AfriDoc-MT, with a smallest model of 4B [@yu2026afriquellm]. Sentence-level alignment of long outputs goes back to Gale and Church [@gale1993program] and Vecalign [@thompson2019vecalign]; repetition and degeneration in long generation are classic phenomena [@holtzman2020curious; @welleck2020unlikelihood] that reappear as the dominant failure of our whole-document decoding. FormatRL applies GRPO to document-level translation of structured XML documents [@song2025formatrl] and LongWriter-Zero uses RL for ultra-long writing [@wu2025longwriterzero]; we found no RL study of end-to-end translation of long documents with a small model.

**Reinforcement learning for translation.** GRPO [@shao2024deepseekmath] and its analyses [@guo2025deepseekr1; @liu2025understanding; @yu2025dapo] (group-relative advantages, length and standard-deviation normalisation, KL estimators [@schulman2020approximating]) underlie R1-style translation work: MT-R1-Zero (3B and 7B models) [@feng2025mtr1zero], R1-T1 [@he2025r1t1], DeepTrans and ExTrans (7B backbones) [@wang2025deeptrans; @wang2025extrans], Seed-X (7B) [@cheng2025seedx], Hunyuan-MT and HY-MT1.5 [@zheng2025hunyuanmt; @zheng2025hymt15], and TranslateGemma, which applies RL with a reward-model ensemble (MetricX-QE, an AutoMQM-style QE model, chrF and others) to Gemma 3 at 4B, 12B and 27B [@finkelstein2026translategemma]. Closest to our setting are RL fine-tuning of NLLB-600M/1.3B with a round-trip chrF++ reward, covering Wolof and Dyula among other languages [@attia2026roundtrip], reference-free GRPO on NLLB with LaBSE and COMET-Kiwi over 13 languages [@garciaestrada2026reffree], and RL with a chrF reward for unseen-language translation [@hu2026rlunseen]. Earlier work optimised metrics directly [@shen2016mrt] or used them for preference optimisation [@xu2024cpo] and decoding [@freitag2022mbr; @fernandes2022qualityaware; @eikema2020map]. Label-free RL signals such as agreement among samples [@zuo2025ttrl; @wang2023selfconsistency] are the analogue of our consensus reward. Length curricula for RL exist for reasoning [@song2025fastcurl; @hammoud2025trainlong]; we found none for translation.

**Metrics, reward hacking and quality estimation.** COMET-family and MetricX metrics [@rei2020comet; @rei2022cometkiwi; @guerreiro2024xcomet; @juraska2024metricx24] are strong on high-resource languages and less reliable for low-resource ones; the size of a metric difference that matters is metric-specific and thresholds may not transfer across languages [@kocmi2024metricsmaze], chrF/chrF++ and spBLEU remain the surface reference [@popovic2017chrfpp; @post2018call], and bootstrap resampling is the usual significance test [@koehn2004significance]. Optimising a learned reward overoptimises it [@gao2023scaling; @karwowski2023goodhart; @krakovna2020specification], ensembles mitigate this [@coste2024ensembles], and length is a classic exploit [@singhal2024long]. For translation specifically, WALAR documents self-generated references, non-translations, over- and under-translation and wrong-language output as hacks of a QE reward and adds word- and language-alignment rewards [@liu2026walar]; MiLMMT-style work gates a two-QE reward by language identification [@han2026milmmtrl]; language identification for low-resource languages is the job of GlotLID [@kargaran2023glotlid]. We extend this line to African languages, a 270M model and long documents, and add measurements of detector false-positive rates against human text.

**Small models, multilinguality, forgetting.** Gemma 3 and its 270M variant [@gemmateam2025gemma3; @google2025gemma270m] are positioned for task-specific fine-tuning; small multilingual models face the curse of multilinguality [@conneau2020unsupervised; @chang2024multilinguality], unfair tokenisation of low-resource scripts [@petrov2023tokenizer] and language-sampling choices [@chung2023unimax]. Weight-space interpolation between a fine-tuned and a base model recovers robustness [@wortsman2022robust], and averaging or combining fine-tuned weights is a general tool [@wortsman2022soups; @ilharco2023editing]; replay mitigates forgetting [@rolnick2019experience], LoRA learns and forgets less than full fine-tuning [@biderman2024lora], and RL forgets less than SFT and changes small subnetworks [@shenfeld2025razor; @mukherjee2025subnetworks]. Whether RL generalises where SFT memorises [@chu2025sft] or only sharpens what the base model already contains [@yue2025doesrl] is open for reasoning, and we know of no controlled comparison for translation; reference-free RL has been proposed as a route where parallel data are scarce [@liu2026walar], and Section 8 contributes one observational data point (RL against added parallel data, for two African languages) and a small controlled ablation (four languages, one seed per arm).

**Positioning and the novelty claim.** To our knowledge no earlier work applies RL with a quality-estimation reward ensemble to a decoder-only model of about 270M parameters for English-to-African translation over 77 targets, or trains such a model with an output-length curriculum up to 2,048-token inputs; in the wording we are prepared to defend, this is the first *open* 270M-parameter model fine-tuned with RL against an African-language quality estimator on prompts of up to 2,048 tokens. Neighbouring work limits what we can claim: RL for MT with small models exists (NLLB-600M/1.3B), RL for MT with a QE-ensemble reward exists (TranslateGemma, 4B and up), reward-hacking defences for MT-RL have been studied (WALAR), and small African MT models (TranslatePsy-AfriSLM) and African document-level benchmarks (AfriDoc-MT) exist. We therefore claim neither first RL for low-resource MT nor first small model nor first reward-hacking analysis. *On language coverage we make no claim of priority.* NLLB-200 supports 55 African languages [@nllb2022nllb]; MADLAD-400 lists about 85 African entries [@kudugunta2023madlad] and TranslateGemma about 60 [@finkelstein2026translategemma] (both our manual tallies of the papers' tables, unverified); Omnilingual MT (Meta, 2026) reports a far wider language list that we did not verify. What we claim is narrower and conditional (Section 6.8): to our knowledge the open model with the widest African target-language coverage *below 1B parameters* (77 languages against 55 for NLLB-200-600M), which says nothing about quality, where our model is below NLLB-200-600M; and 33 languages absent from FLORES-200, 17 of which we could not find in NLLB-200, MADLAD-400 or the TranslateGemma language lists. In addition, our own long-document result (Section 6.4) is narrower than the goal that motivated it: the RL-trained model translates 2,048- and 4,096-token documents only with sentence-level chunked decoding and its single-pass translation works to about 512 tokens; the science-stage model finishes whole documents of up to about 2,048 tokens but omits 13-30% of the sentences, and NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM with a sentence-by-sentence wrapper are 1-4 chrF++ ahead of it (Section 6.10). The literature search behind these statements (about 28 searches, 2026-10-22 and 2026-10-23, plus a coverage survey of 35 systems, Appendix G) is recorded in `paper/lit/notes_*.md` and `reports/data/language_coverage_claim_check.md`; it should be repeated on Google Scholar, the ACL Anthology (WMT and EMNLP 2026) and Hugging Face papers before submission.

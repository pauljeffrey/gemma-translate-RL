## 6 Results

### 6.1 From SFT to RL: sentence-level translation

We lead with the evidence least affected by the exposure of the test sets (Section 6.9a) and keep `all` and `no_pair` visible in {{tref:t_main}}. On the `clean` rows (the English sentence was never in the SFT text; 48 FLORES+ sentences per language) RL raises the FLORES+ macro chrF++ over 45 languages from 25.1 to 31.7 (+6.62 [5.83, 7.24]), and the gain is as large or larger on NTREX-128 (+4.09 [3.87, 4.32]), SmolSent (+7.06 [6.17, 7.70]) and GlotStoryBook (+8.26 [7.58, 8.87]), equal on MAFAND-MT (+3.66 [3.03, 4.16]) and smaller but positive on SALT (+2.31 [0.85, 3.57]). On FLORES+ devtest restricted to the rows whose reference pair the model never saw (`no_pair`, 96% of the rows; the headline of the earlier drafts) RL raises the macro chrF++ over 45 languages from 28.7 to 34.1, a gain of 5.42 [5.22, 5.61], and spBLEU from 9.2 to 12.4 (+3.19 [2.97, 3.38]). The exposure raises the *level* of both models: on FLORES+ the macro over all rows minus the macro over the `clean` rows is +3.8 chrF++ for SFT-final and +2.6 for RL-final (about 3.2 and 2.0 after the 0.6 that the harder clean sentences cost the untrained base model), +6.4 and +4.6 on SmolSent, and the `no_pair` headline levels (28.7 and 34.1) stay about 3.6 and 2.4 above the `clean` level; the headline *differences* are less affected because both models share the SFT exposure. All 45 FLORES+ languages improve and none drops; on `no_pair` rows the same pattern holds on NTREX-128 (+3.86 [3.72, 4.01]), MAFAND-MT (+3.66 [3.12, 4.11]), SmolSent (+5.36 [5.11, 5.57]), GlotStoryBook (+8.16) and IbomMT (+11.04), whose Ibibio and Efik reference sets are outside the usual benchmarks. **IbomMT is two languages and only one of them is clean of the reward calibration:** the Efik thresholds of the reward were fitted on the IbomMT test set (997 of its 1,012 sentences, Section 6.9a), so the Efik gain (SFT 18.5 to RL 31.2, +12.70 [11.67, 13.56]) is not an independent result, whereas the Ibibio gain (12.9 to 22.3, +9.37 [8.69, 9.99]) is unaffected; the macro of +11.04 mixes the two ({{tref:t_main}}, {{fref:fig2_per_language_gain}}). The *finished rate* (the completion ends inside the 768-token budget) rises from 90.4% to 100.0%, and the GlotLID right-language rate from 95.2% to 96.7%.

Two qualifications belong next to this headline. (i) *Budget.* A looping completion is scored as it stands, so the SFT model is penalised more at a generous budget: at the 320-token budget of our earlier reports the same comparison is 30.4 to 34.1 (+3.70); about 1.7 of the 5.4 points of the 768-token gain are the SFT model's runaway tail, and the remaining 3.7 still contain the 10% of SFT sentences that loop inside 320 tokens. Part of what RL buys is therefore *learning to stop*, and our data cannot separate it from translation quality. (ii) *Groups.* Gains are larger where the SFT model is weaker ({{tref:t_groups}}): +4.95 [4.75, 5.14] for the 40 QE-validated FLORES+ languages, +11.22 [10.68, 11.75] for the two consensus-only languages (Kabuverdianu, Makhuwa), +14.59 for the one Creole in the table, and only +1.26 for Afrikaans and Malagasy. On the *primary* benchmark of every language (a coverage summary, not a like-for-like score) the macro gain is +4.97 [4.78, 5.16] for the 42 QE-validated, +7.93 [7.55, 8.36] for the 9 blend and +7.69 [7.30, 8.07] for the 13 consensus-only languages that have a human-reference set (Appendix A, {{tref:t_master}}). The consensus-only languages, for which the reward contained *no learned quality metric*, thus improved at least as much as the validated ones. *The largest gains.* On the 44 languages that also have an NLLB row (the identical-sentence pipeline of Section 6.8, `no_pair`) the largest gains of RL-final over SFT-final are Kabuverdianu +14.2, Kabiye +14.1, Kamba +12.1, Luo +11.9, Nuer +11.4, Kanuri +11.0, Bambara +9.2 and Kimbundu +8.9 chrF++, against a mean gain of +5.35 and a median of +4.38 ({{tref:t_skew_langs}}; the generation-run table of this section gives +14.6, +14.2 and +11.5 for Kabuverdianu, Kabiye and Nuer, differing from this pipeline by at most 0.53 chrF++ for any of the 45 languages). Part of the largest gains is repair, not new knowledge: the SFT model finished only 34% of the Kabiye, 51% of the Nuer, 70% of the Kamba and 76% of the Kabuverdianu translations (RL-final 100%), Kanuri has no SFT data and its gain is a stopping effect, and in the controlled ablation of Section 8.4 the gain on the sentences that every system finished is much smaller than on all sentences (+1.13 against +7.36 from the weak start, +2.23 against +5.72 from SFT-final; four languages, one run per arm). The gains of Luo, Bambara and Kimbundu, where SFT finished 85-91%, cannot be explained by repair alone. Kabuverdianu is both the largest gain over SFT (+14.2) and the largest remaining gap to NLLB-200 3.3B (21.0), and we show both.

### 6.4 Long documents: what the length curriculum did and did not achieve

The goal of the curriculum was to make a 270 M model translate documents of up to 2,048 tokens. {{fref:fig4_length_bins}} and {{tref:t_longdoc_chrf}} show the result and it is mixed.

*Whole-document generation by RL-final does not work beyond about 512 source tokens, whatever the decoding setting* (this subsection is about the SFT-final and RL-final checkpoints; the science-stage checkpoints and other systems are evaluated in Section 6.10). With greedy decoding the final model finishes 98 / 97 / 92 / 65 / 42 / 31% of the documents of the bins b64, b256, b512, b1024, b2048, b4096, 0 / 1 / 13 / 53 / 73 / 73% of its outputs contain a loop that the reference does not, and chrF++ against the NLLB reference falls from 47.6 to 47.7, 47.7, 29.7, 18.2 and 9.8 ({{tref:t_longdoc_fin}}). This reproduces the reward measured on b2048 prompts (greedy total reward -1.95, 7% "ok"). The SFT model fails earlier: whole-document SFT output is already degraded at b256 (74% finished) and reaches 32.5, 17.0, 11.1 and 6.9 chrF++ at b512 to b4096. RL therefore *did* extend the range of single-pass translation, from about 256 to about 512 source tokens (b512: 32.5 to 47.7), and it improved every longer bin, but it did not make single-pass translation of 1,024- or 2,048-token inputs work. Repetition penalties, frequency penalty, temperature from 0.3 to 1.0, best-of-N and no-repeat n-grams trade loops for *truncation*: at T = 0.7 documents finish (99 / 95 / 78% at b1024 / b2048 / b4096) but the output is only 77%, 56% and 33% as long as the reference, so chrF++ stays at 26-37 for b1024-b2048 and 14.5 at b4096. We did not evaluate the intermediate checkpoints on AfriLongDoc, so the gain in single-pass range cannot be attributed to the b2048 stage rather than to the earlier stages.

*Chunking with the same model works.* Translating sentence by sentence (greedy) and re-decoding the 1.0% of units that do not emit the end token by best-of-four at T = 0.7 ("sentfix") gives chrF++ of 47.6, 49.0, 53.5, 55.6, 58.4 and 60.1 for the six bins, 100% sentence coverage and 0-4% loops; against the *human* SmolDoc references it reaches 47.2 / 49.1 / 50.6 chrF++ on short documents and on stitched documents of about 512 and 1,024 tokens (six languages) and 52.4 on stitched documents of about 2,048 tokens (four languages), where whole-document greedy decoding reaches 18.2 ({{tref:t_longdoc_human}}). Its AfriCOMET is 0.691 at b64 and 0.659 at b4096 (reference-based, aligned chunks), a mild decline. It is also faster in wall-clock time on a shared GPU (1,196 s against 1,912 s for all 9,506 items). So *RL-final can translate documents of 2,048 and even 4,096 tokens, but only when decoding is chunked by sentence* (after the science stage the whole-document mode works to about 2,048 tokens but omits text, Section 6.10); windows of 256 tokens are an acceptable alternative (tied with sentences against human references at 1,024 and 2,048 tokens, 1.0-1.3 chrF++ lower against NLLB, twice the loop rate). The final model is 2.1-3.3 chrF++ above the SFT model under the same chunking, and the SFT model does not stop reliably even then (13-48% finished at b512-b4096, loops in 42-59%).

Four limits keep these numbers honest. (i) *Pseudo-reference bias.* The NLLB reference is itself sentence-by-sentence, which favours chunked hypotheses by construction; the model agrees more with NLLB (65.9 chrF++ at b1024 on five languages) than MADLAD-400 does (62.7), and NLLB-referenced scores are 1-16 chrF++ above the human-referenced scores for all 12 languages with both. We therefore quote the human-reference column for absolute quality (47 to 52 chrF++ from short to ~2,048-token documents) and use the NLLB column only to compare strategies; the ordering (chunked far above whole-document beyond ~512 tokens; sentence at least as good as window) is the same against MADLAD, against humans and under reference-free QE, but "sentence much better than window" is not established. (ii) Absolute chrF++ rises with the bin because the bins are different texts; the AfriCOMET column is the comparable one. (iii) The human documents beyond ~680 tokens are stitched from unrelated ones, so none of this measures discourse quality (pronouns, terminology across paragraphs). (iv) The language set (8 / 12 / 16) is fixed and the intervals resample documents only (35-100 per bin).

The *training* reward tells the same story. The total RL reward of whole-document greedy output is 0.73, 0.60, -0.25, -1.53, -1.95, -2.00 for b64 to b4096, that of sentfix 0.77, 0.83, 0.12, -0.19, -0.46, -1.03, and of the NLLB translations themselves under the same detectors 1.03, 0.89, 0.31, 0.10, -0.20, -0.96 ({{tref:t_longdoc_reward}}): at b1024 and beyond, "about -0.2 to -1.0" is the practical ceiling of the v1 reward because its repetition detector fails good translations (Section 7.4). For T = 1 *rollouts* of the b1024 and b2048 bins the groups are almost flat (the within-group standard deviation of the reward is 0.18 and 0.11): the sampled members all stop, all truncate, and are all about equally bad, so GRPO has little to learn from (`reports/longdoc_reward_v2_2026-10-22.md` section 0).

### 6.5 Decoding

For short text, beam search is the only family that improves on greedy decoding: macro chrF++ over 20 languages (200 `no_pair` sentences each) is 32.52 for greedy, 33.18 for beam 4 (+0.66 [+0.55, +0.78]) and 33.26 for beam 8, with AfriCOMET 0.6230, 0.6338 and 0.6344. Beam search loops where greedy does not (0.1% for greedy, 0.6% for beam 4, concentrated in Nuer and Kabiye), so a greedy fallback when the beam output is unfinished, repetitive or empty is recommended (33.20). Consensus decoding (MBR over 8 samples at T 0.5-0.7) gains about 0.5 chrF++ but no spBLEU; single-sample sampling is monotonically *worse* (T = 0.3 / 0.5 / 0.7: -0.13 / -0.35 / -0.79), and the Gemma default sampler shipped in `generation_config.json` (T 1, top-$k$ 64, top-$p$ 0.95) costs 1.40 chrF++; repetition penalty 1.1 costs 1.48 and `no_repeat_ngram_size` 3 costs 1.06 and removes 80% of the copied numbers ({{tref:t_decoding}}, {{fref:fig6_decoding}}). Greedy output is nevertheless repetitive in the weakest languages (a repeated word 3-gram in 52% of Kabiye and Nuer sentences against 2.7% in the references), and removing the repetition by sampling costs more chrF++ than it gains (`reports/generation_config_study_2026-10-22.md`). For the SFT model the picture is different (it loops in 10.9% of sentences; T 0.5 helps its weak group by +1.40, MBR over 16 samples gives +2.45), which is one more way in which RL changed the model beyond a shift in chrF++.

### 6.6 The science stage and its guardrails

A short supervised stage on AfriScience-MT with replay improves science translation sharply but, in its plain form, costs the languages that RL had improved with its own consensus signal ({{tref:t_sft_stage_v1}}): with the first mixture (science plus replay), the sentence chrF++ on the AfriScience dev set rises from 45.65 (RL-final) to 47.7-48.1 and the paper-level chrF from 36.0 to 45-46, and the paper-level finished rate from 74.7% to 89-92% (interpolation weights 0.5 to 1.0; at 0.3 the gains are smaller), but the 19 weak-blend languages lose 0.4 to 2.3 chrF++ at every interpolation weight ($\alpha=0.7$: -1.04 [-1.39, -0.68]), and an L2-SP pull towards the start weights does not fix it. The cause is the replay of human references that pull the model back from what its own consensus signal had taught it. A second mixture (halved weak-language human replay, unfiltered self-distilled anchors, RL-archive translations and 13.5% long-document distillation; "A2W") brings the weak-blend loss at $\alpha=0.7$ to -0.36 [-0.58, -0.16] while keeping the science gain (sentence 47.97, paper-level chrF 47.81, finished 92.0%) and passes the guardrail rule ({{tref:t_sft_stage_v2}}, {{fref:fig7_sft_stage_guardrail}}). On the *test* split, read once, it moves the sentence chrF++ from 44.90 to 47.13, the paper-level chrF from 33.05 to 45.76 and the paper-level finished rate from 72% to 94%. Adding the NLLB-3.3B teacher data (4.47 M tokens, 27 languages, non-commercial) did not beat A2W (its best variant is 0.36 chrF++ lower on science sentences) and was not adopted; adding the IbomMT *training* data brought no gain for Ibibio or Efik (RL-final 22.27 / 31.27 chrF++, the mixture without IbomMT data 22.07 / 30.79, the mixture with it 21.92 / 30.66 at $\alpha=0.5$ and 21.58 / 30.02 at $\alpha=0.7$). Note that the paper-level (single-pass whole-document) gains of this stage come from supervised long-document distillation, not from RL. This is the stage that makes native long output finish (Section 6.10).

### 6.7 The final run (final2)

*What was run and how it ended.* The final run (science and general prompts in the ratio 70:30, 77 languages, Section 4.7) started from the interpolated science-stage model A2W ($\alpha=0.7$) on 2026-10-22 at 13:00 UTC with 700 steps planned and **ended at step 401**. The project lead had fixed four stop criteria in advance, and at the evaluation of step 350 all four held: no gain of the evaluation reward of +0.03 or more, sentence-level chrF++ within $\pm 0.3$, the paper-level chrF++ (mean of the evaluations at steps 300 and 350) less than 1.0 above the reference value, and the weak-language dev chrF++ not up by 0.3; no forgetting criterion triggered. The wording of the criteria is not in a repository file (they are quoted from the project lead's instruction) and the reference point is not stated there. Measured from the evaluation of step 250, the start of the 100-step window, all four hold: the evaluation reward was -0.586 and -0.566 at steps 300 and 350 against -0.525 at step 250, sentence chrF++ 48.82 and 48.86 against 48.84 (48.79 at the start), paper-level chrF++ 45.36 on average against 45.24, and weak-language dev chrF++ 25.32 and 25.26 against 25.41. Measured from step 0, the paper-level criterion would not hold (45.36 against 44.02, +1.34; TODO(owner: confirm the reference point of the paper-level criterion)). A linear wind-down of the learning rate over 50 steps (steps 351-401; 3.75e-7 at step 351 to 7.5e-9 at step 401) was then started (written 2026-10-23 at 13:42 UTC), followed by the final evaluation at step 401 and the push of the weights.

*Interruptions and settings.* The step series of the final lineage is continuous from step 1 to 401 ({{fref:fig9_final2}} marks the events). (i) A planned restart after the step-150 checkpoint (the process was ended by SIGTERM at 21:22 UTC on 2026-10-22 and restarted twenty seconds later): the KL guard, which had cut the learning-rate scale to 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125 between steps 121 and 145, was reset (scale 1.0 at step 151, 0.5 again from step 155), and the padding ("stuffed output") detector stopped firing: it had marked 3.2-4.7% of the completions of every 25-step block of steps 1-150 as hard failures and marked none in steps 151-401, so the hard-failure share of the training batches fell from 0.056-0.061 to 0.013-0.017 (25-step blocks) and the mean training reward rose by about 0.2 at that step ({{fref:fig9_final2}}, top left). The configuration of the restart points to version 3 of the anchors (Section 7.3); we infer from this change in the metrics, not from a log line, that version 3 was in force from step 151 on and the first 150 steps ran with the earlier detector (TODO(owner: confirm)). The training reward is therefore not comparable across step 150 and is not a quality measure; the same holds for the evaluation reward before and after step 150. From step 182 the per-language draw weights of `LANG_WEIGHTS.json` (the dry-run proposals of the adaptive controller at step 150, applied by the project lead) were in force; the controller itself never changed a weight (dry run, 0 updates). (ii) Two out-of-memory kills (2026-10-23 at 04:22:56 and 04:49:17 UTC) because other jobs on the shared GPU had filled its memory; the run was resumed from the step-250 checkpoint each time with a smaller scoring micro-batch (24,576, then 12,288, then 6,144 tokens per forward), which does not change the optimisation, and steps 251-267 of the first attempt were discarded (the third process, which produced steps 251-401 including the wind-down, is the one reported). The operator-set learning-rate scale was 0.75 from step 251 (written 03:13 UTC; 0.5 before), the KL guard was reset at the restart (scale 1.0 from step 251, 0.5 again from step 341). The run is therefore not a single uninterrupted trajectory, and we have not checked how the sampling streams of the resumed steps relate to those of the discarded attempt.

*Time.* The 401 steps of the final lineage took 26.2 hours of step time (median 218 s per step; 197 s in steps 1-150, 205 s in 151-250, 283 s in 251-401, where the GPU was shared and the micro-batch smaller), 26.6 hours of wall-clock between the first and the last step of the lineage excluding the 112 minutes lost between the step-250 evaluation and the first resumed step, and 28.4 hours between the first and the last record of the metrics file (Appendix F). The last process, steps 251-401, ran 45,456 s (12.6 h).

*Result: the sentence-level gain over the start is small, the run mostly made the model stable and finishing.* {{tref:t_final2}} and {{fref:fig9_final2}} give the evaluations (every 50 steps, and at the last step). On the AfriScience dev sentences chrF++ is 48.92 at step 401 against 48.79 at the start (+0.13; BLEU 25.74 against 25.46; the nine evaluations after step 0 lie between 48.63 and 48.93 with a standard deviation of 0.10; the per-language changes are zul +0.43, amh +0.31, yor +0.06, lug -0.01, hau -0.02, nso -0.03). On the general dev set (19 languages, 760 sentences) it is 42.04 against 41.97 (+0.07; 13 of 19 languages up, largest Fon +1.12 and Igbo +0.77, smallest Swahili -1.23 and Zulu -0.88), on the weak-language dev set (13 languages, 60 sentences each) 25.28 against 24.90 (+0.38; 12 of 13 languages up, Mossi +1.08, Umbundu +0.64, Kabiye -0.21), but that series peaked at 25.55 at step 100 and drifted down by 0.27 afterwards, and with 60 sentences per language (noise about 3 chrF++ per language, Section 8.2) we do not consider a change of this size established. We computed no confidence intervals for these changes (the per-sentence outputs are not part of the metrics file); the spread of the evaluations above is the only noise estimate. At paper level (150 development papers, 25 in each of six languages of 542-1,346 source tokens, translated in a single pass) the finished rate went from 0.927 (139 of 150) to 1.000 within 50 steps, stayed at 0.993-1.000 until step 300 and was 0.967 (145 of 150) at steps 350 and 401; paper-level chrF++ went from 44.02 to 46.93 at step 50 and then 45.47, 45.27, 46.49, 45.24, 46.11, 44.61, 45.00 and 44.37 at step 401 (+0.35 at the end, standard deviation of the series 0.85). Per language at paper level (end against start, one 25-document sample each) Luganda +1.91, Zulu +1.36 and Amharic +0.94 are up and Hausa -1.58 and Yoruba -0.56 down. The overall reading is that, as in phase 2c, RL moved nothing at sentence level beyond the noise of these sets (the +0.13 on the sentence dev set is slightly larger than the standard deviation of that series, 0.10, and we do not call it established), and that the paper-level finished rate, which RL had already moved to about 1.0 in the first 50 steps, ended slightly below its best. The main effects of the run are the ones that the earlier sections describe: the finish-rate and loop repair of the science-stage model and a stable optimisation with the amended reward. The benchmark of Section 6.10 shows that finishing is not the same as translating everything (the step-250 checkpoint covers 87% of the source sentences at about 1,024 tokens and 70% at about 2,048).

*Evaluation reward.* The mean reward on 192 held-out prompts was -0.783 at the first evaluation (step 25), -0.443 at step 175 (the best, *not* kept: only the latest weights were pushed, Section 13), -0.516 to -0.586 at steps 200-350 and -0.560 at step 401 (mean QE of the scored completions 0.544, finished rate 0.9375). The series is flat after step 200 within a standard deviation of 0.03, and the step-175 maximum follows the change of the padding detector at step 150, so that earlier values are not comparable. By length bin the final evaluation reward is +0.10 (b64), -0.21 (b256), -0.48 (b512), -0.62 (b1024) and -1.92 (b2048); the b2048 prompts stayed between -1.82 and -1.95 at all 17 evaluations, i.e. close to the hard-failure floor of -2: RL with this reward did not teach the 2,048-token windows, in line with Section 6.4. By domain the final reward is -0.86 on science and -0.15 on general prompts.

*Specific languages.* The Amharic sentence chrF++ of the AfriScience dev set is 25.59 at step 401 against 25.28 at the start and 25.65 at step 250, and Amharic at paper level 23.50 against 22.57 (24.71 at step 250): the small Amharic gain of the stage was already in place at step 250 and was not extended (Section 9.1). *Stability.* Over the 401 steps the KL to the start model had a median of 0.096 (10th and 90th percentile 0.049 and 0.152, maximum 0.267; 45 of 401 steps above the guard level of 0.15, 44 of them before step 351 and one in the wind-down) with the KL coefficient 0.1; the pre-clip gradient norm had a median of 0.71 and a maximum of 3.45 (64 steps above the clip at 1.0, 3 of them in the wind-down). The greedy member, with its advantage computed from the sampled members only, no longer dominates the gradient. The wind-down itself changed nothing visible: the mean KL of its steps (median 0.101) and the evaluations at steps 350, 400 and 401 are within the noise of the evaluations before it.

### 6.8 Comparison with other systems on identical sentences

Section 6.2 compared our model with the tables printed in other papers. That comparison cannot include a learned African metric, because **AfriCOMET is not published for almost any of the baseline systems**. We audited the 43 baseline models of our published-baseline file (full matrix in Appendix G, {{tref:t_model_comparison_comet_audit}}): an AfriCOMET score appears in a paper for exactly two of them, NLLB-200-distilled-600M and its pruned variant AfriNLLB, and there with an older checkpoint (`africomet-mtl` v1.0, 9 FLORES-200 languages, Wolof outside the encoder's languages) than the one we use (`africomet-stl-1.1`) [@moslem2026afrinllb]. What the recent African-MT papers report is SSA-COMET [@li2025ssacomet], a different metric: for 34 of the 43 models (25 via TranslatePsy-AfriSLM [@gritta2026translatepsy], 19 languages with both directions averaged as far as we can tell; 9 more only via AfriqueLLM's English-to-X table [@yu2026afriquellm], 24 languages, checkpoint not named). For NLLB-200-distilled-1.3B, the 54.5B mixture-of-experts model, Aya-101, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Gemma 2 27B and Llama 3.1 70B we found no COMET-family number at all. A joint chrF++ and AfriCOMET table over the baselines is therefore impossible from the literature, and we built one from our own scoring.

*Protocol.* All systems we could run were scored with one metric stack on the *same* FLORES+ devtest English-to-X sentences, restricted to the `no_pair` rows (the sentences whose reference pair our SFT never saw; 170 to 1,012 per language; the `clean` rows, 48 per language whose English side is not in the SFT text, are reported in the appendix tables): chrF++ (sacreBLEU, word order 2, NFC), spBLEU, reference-based AfriCOMET-STL-1.1 and, for 22 languages, reference-based SSA-COMET-MTL. The systems are our five checkpoints (RL-final; SFT-final; A2W, the start of the final run; the step-250 checkpoint of the final run; and the final checkpoint of that run, step 401, added after the run ended), NLLB-200-distilled-600M and NLLB-200-3.3B (the CTranslate2 beam-4 translations of Section 7.5, re-scored), and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B (all 19 of its languages) and 2B (only 9 of them: the run was stopped when the GPU budget was cut). The 2B model's weights alone, like those of TranslatePsy-4B, AfriqueGemma-4B and Gemma-3-4B-it, did not fit the 6 GB left to us on the shared GPU; those models are *not* in the computed rows, and their published numbers stay in a separate column. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals over sentences (1,000 resamples, one resample shared by all systems, hence paired); they do **not** contain language-sampling noise, and a macro is given only for systems that cover every language of the set. As a check of the stack, our chrF++ for NLLB-200-3.3B on the whole devtest differs from the published value by -0.17 on average over 43 languages (mean absolute difference 0.40; the largest is Yoruba, +3.02, caused by Unicode normalisation: 28% of the Yoruba references are in decomposed form).

*Headline comparison: 42 languages without Amharic and Tigrinya, with the two reported alongside.* Amharic and Tigrinya, the only Ge'ez-script languages in our set, are, together with Kabuverdianu (a creole, gap 21.0, which we keep in), the largest single gaps to NLLB-200 3.3B (Amharic 19.0 and Tigrinya 11.0 chrF++; RL-final 19.0 and 13.8, NLLB-200 3.3B 38.0 and 24.7, NLLB-200-distilled-600M 37.3 and 22.4). On the 44 languages for which RL-final, the final run, NLLB-200 3.3B and NLLB-200-distilled-600M all have a `no_pair` row (the 43 languages below plus Kanuri, for which our NLLB re-run exists but no published NLLB number does), the macro chrF++ is 34.25, 34.48, 38.09 and 35.94; without the two Ge'ez-script languages (42 languages) it is 35.10, 35.32, 38.42 and 36.23. *How skewed is the gap?* The mean gap is carried by a few languages, and we say so wherever it is quoted. *Limits of this comparison.* (i) NLLB is run sentence by sentence with beam 4 (CTranslate2, int8 for the 3.3B model), ours greedy; beam 4 gave our own model +0.66 chrF++ on 20 languages in Section 6.5, so part of the gap may be decoding, but we did not re-run either side with the other's decoding. ### 6.9 Benchmark-based pairwise comparison of SFT-final and RL-final (stand-in for a human evaluation)

We have no native-speaker evaluation of our outputs (Section 11). To test the claim that RL *improved* the model with something other than a macro average of one metric, we compare RL-final with the SFT-final model it started from **sentence by sentence** on the human-reference benchmarks, under five metrics, and ask how often each metric prefers the RL translation. This measures agreement with human references under automatic metrics; it is not a human evaluation, and we word every result as "preferred by metric X in Y% of sentences". The analysis (agent E1; `reports/pairwise_benchmark_comparison_2026-10-23.md`; scripts `gemma_mt/scripts/pairwise_*.py`) uses the hypotheses of both models for all 161,272 English-to-X sentences of the eight benchmarks of Section 6.1 (66 languages with at least 20 `no_pair` sentences; greedy decoding, 768 tokens; the run reproduces the corpus-level chrF++ of Section 6.1 within 0.01-0.2 per benchmark).

*Method.* For every sentence and metric the RL output *wins* if its score exceeds the SFT output's by more than a threshold (1 chrF++ point; 0.01 for the COMET-family metrics, whose scale is about 0-1), *loses* if it is lower by more than the threshold, and otherwise ties; identical outputs are ties. "RL preferred" is wins divided by wins plus losses. The metrics are sentence-level chrF++ and the learned metrics AfriCOMET-STL-1.1 and SSA-COMET-MTL with the human reference, plus the reference-free QE variants (AfriCOMET-QE, SSA-COMET-MTL without reference). The learned metrics were run on the 51 languages that AfriCOMET covers and have at least 20 `no_pair` sentences, on every `clean` row plus at most 400 rows per (benchmark, language): 79,062 sentence pairs per metric. Intervals are paired sentence-bootstrap intervals (1,000 resamples); language-level statements use the bootstrap interval of each language's mean difference; the sign tests of the report are optimistic because several benchmarks translate the same English sentences into every language, and we do not use them.

*Which metrics are independent of the reward.* RL-final (phase 2c, the model of Sections 6.1-6.6) was trained with AfriCOMET-QE as the only learned metric: the `rl2c` configuration names `masakhane/africomet-qe-stl-1.1` as its sole scorer, and the ensemble that adds SSA-COMET-MTL (decision D-55, 2026-10-21) exists only in the final run and is off by default, so SSA-COMET is **not** in the reward of RL-final or of any phase before it. Consequently chrF++ against human references and SSA-COMET with reference are independent evidence for the RL-final comparison; AfriCOMET with reference shares its model family (encoder and training data) with the reward model and is not independent in the strict sense, and the QE metrics are the reward model itself or its sibling. The AfriCOMET-QE reward model also appears in the table, as a check on reward hacking.

**Headline** ({{tref:t_pw_headline}}, {{fref:figpw1_wtl}}). **What this does and does not show.** (i) These are metric preferences: chrF++ and the learned metrics agree on a sentence's winner in only 64-73% of the cases and the learned metrics were validated by their authors on a subset of our languages, with one reference; a rater study could still differ. (ii) AfriCOMET with reference is not independent of the reward; chrF++ and SSA-COMET with reference are, and they point the same way. (iii) FLORES+ and NTREX-128 are public and the SFT text held the English side of 95% of the FLORES+ sentences and, for seven languages, the reference translation itself (Section 6.9a); the `no_pair` subset controls for the pair leak only, the `clean` subset (48 sentences per language) for both, and the preference is similar on both (63.7% and 61.6%), so the conclusion does not change, but the levels do. (iv) The benchmarks are news, encyclopaedic and children's-story text, while the training data are religious and pivot-translated; the smallest gains are where the domains are closest, and nothing here tests user text, science or dialogue. (v) Greedy decoding only; one reference per sentence; equal weight per language; the learned metrics were run on a sample (all `clean` rows and at most 400 rows per benchmark and language). (vi) Only the step from SFT-final to RL-final (phases 1 to 2c) is compared this way with the learned metrics; the science-stage checkpoints were not, and the final run's checkpoint was compared with RL-final by chrF++ on four benchmarks and with the learned reference-based metrics on NTREX-128, MAFAND-MT, SmolSent and FLORES+ (Section 6.7, Appendix H), not on SALT, GlotStoryBook, IbomMT or FLORES-MOZ.

**Leakage audit of the RL prompts.** The pairwise comparison and the `clean` subset control for leakage into the *SFT* text (the models saw the English side of 95% of the FLORES+ devtest sentences, 91% of SmolSent, 84% of SALT, 40% of NTREX-128, 22% of MAFAND-MT and 7% of GlotStoryBook, by the contamination study of Section 3.6). The RL prompt pools had not been audited, so we did that for this paper (CPU only, `gemma_mt/scripts/p3_rl_leakage_audit.py`, result `reports/data/rl_leakage_audit_2026-10-23.json`): the English side of every benchmark row (23,042 distinct sentences, 161,272 rows) against the English windows of the 77 sentence-level and document-level RL pools (5,940,000 prompt windows, drawn from FineWeb-Edu and Wikipedia text) and of the 77 science pools of the final run (137,172 windows), with the definitions and normalisation of the SFT contamination study (NFKC, case-folding, 8-token n-grams). Exact overlap is rare: 0.69% of the FLORES+ sentences (7 of 1,012; the same sentences back IbomMT and FLORES-MOZ), 0.45% of NTREX-128 (9 of 1,993), 0.46% of MAFAND-MT (81 of 17,737 distinct sentences), 0.23% of SmolSent (2 of 863), 0.2% of SALT (1 of 500) and 1.7% of GlotStoryBook (16 of 937) occur in some RL prompt of some language; at least one 8-gram is shared by 4.8% of the FLORES+ sentences and 1-2% of the others. In the pool of the *same language* as the test row, which is the prompt set that language was trained on, the exact overlap is at most 0.16% of the rows of any benchmark (MAFAND-MT: 31 of 20,008 rows; 0.04-0.10% on the `no_pair` rows: FLORES+ 0.04%, NTREX-128 0.07%, MAFAND-MT 0.09%, SmolSent 0.08%, GlotStoryBook 0.09%, SALT none). On the `clean` rows (English side not in the SFT text) it is 0 of 2,160 FLORES+ rows and 0 of 3,420 SmolSent rows, 48 of 28,824 NTREX-128 rows (0.17%), 12 of 15,633 MAFAND-MT rows (0.08%) and 49 of 3,179 GlotStoryBook rows (1.5%). The science pools of the final run add at most 0.2% on any benchmark. Hence the RL stage trained on the English side of the test sentences about two orders of magnitude less often than the SFT did. The reward scores a completion without a reference translation, but **its thresholds were fitted on human references**: shaping anchors, length and omission windows, diacritic windows, word-ratio and language-identification thresholds on FLORES+ dev and MAFAND dev; for Efik on the IbomMT test set (997 of its 1,012 sentences), which Section 6.1 reports. The final run's anchors were also fitted on the test splits of NTREX-128, SmolSent, SALT, GlotStoryBook, IbomMT and FLORES-MOZ, its long-document limits on SmolDoc and the AfriLongDoc core references, and the five languages with an NLLB signal were chosen by FLORES+ devtest chrF++ gaps. No translation text reached the policy through these, but results on those sets for the final run, and the Efik IbomMT result for RL-final, are not independent tests (Section 6.9a). An exact English overlap of an RL prompt does not hand the policy a translation, which makes prompt overlap a much weaker concern than the parallel leakage of SFT (the pair itself was in the SFT text for 4.1% of the FLORES+ rows, hence the `no_pair` subset). And exact and 8-gram matching misses paraphrases and translations of the same source, and the same English text may enter through the learned reward models' own training data (AfriMTE and SSA-MTE are news-domain; we did not audit them). We therefore treat RL-stage leakage as small but not excluded, and the SFT-stage leakage, shared by both models of the comparison, as the one that matters; it is the reason for the `no_pair` and `clean` subsets and for the finding that the preference is slightly lower, not higher, on `clean` rows by chrF++ (61.6% against 63.7%).

**The same comparison for the final checkpoint (added 2026-10-24).** The final checkpoint (final2, step 401) was compared with RL-final in the same way on NTREX-128, MAFAND-MT, SmolSent and FLORES+ with chrF++, AfriCOMET with reference and SSA-COMET with reference ({{tref:t_final2_pw}} in Section 6.7; per language in Appendix H). The result is the opposite of the SFT-to-RL result in size and, for the learned metrics, in direction: final2 is preferred in 52.4% [52.0, 52.8] of the non-tied sentences by chrF++ and in 47.5% [47.1, 47.8] and 47.6% [47.1, 48.2] by AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference (RL over SFT: 63.7% and about 70%), and the mean learned-metric difference is -0.003, on scales on which RL gained +0.059 over SFT (AfriCOMET and SSA-COMET with reference, macro over 51 languages). We read this as "level"; the two kinds of metric disagree on the sign of a very small difference.

Scaled by each language's share of windows in the sample, the 24 windows stand for about 8,300 windows of the full text, with very wide intervals (Luganda about 900 [510, 1,450]; the others 150 to 4,300 with intervals that span an order of magnitude); this is a sample-based estimate. *One carrier is named* (VERIFIED by streaming): `African-Languages-Lab/multi-open`, as repackaged in `<HF_ORG_LEGACY>/sft-new`, holds FLORES+ rows in Zulu (49 devtest and 47 dev English sentences, 96,150 rows scanned) and Igbo (23 devtest and 36 dev sentences with their Igbo translation, 179,604 rows scanned), which accounts for the Zulu pair flags and about 4% of the Igbo ones. *The reward: thresholds fitted on human references, and five places where a test split entered.* The reward scores a completion without a reference translation, but it has thresholds and statistics fitted on human references (Section 4.3). 1. **RL-final, Efik: the IbomMT test set.** The shaping anchors, the length and omission windows, the diacritic window and the lexical block of Efik were fitted on 997 of the 1,012 IbomMT test sentences, because Efik has no other human reference set (`calibration/shaping_flores_dev_v7.json`, entry `efi`; AUC 0.731). The same Efik entry is in the final run. We have no re-run with Efik calibrated elsewhere (for example on pooled priors), so the size of the effect is unknown; Ibibio, for which nothing was fitted on IbomMT, gains +9.4 against +12.7 for Efik, but the two languages differ in data and reward group and this is not a test.
2. **The final run: anchors fitted on six test splits.** Its constraint anchors (copy, entity, function-word, padding, repetition, boilerplate) were fitted on human units that include, besides FLORES+ dev, SmolDoc and AfriScience dev documents, the test splits of NTREX-128, SmolSent, IbomMT, SALT, FLORES-MOZ and GlotStoryBook ("thresholds only, never training data"). The final run's results on NTREX-128 and SmolSent in Section 6.7 (+0.17 and +0.20 chrF++ over RL-final) are therefore not independent tests; RL-final's reward has no such anchors.
3. **The final run: long-document limits fitted on the evaluation documents.** The limits of the long-document reward (repetition quantile, tail gap) were fitted on 19,770 documents: the SmolDoc human references stitched over 45 languages and the NLLB-3.3B references of the AfriLongDoc core set, which are the evaluation documents of Section 6.10 (statistics only, no text in the policy).
4. **The final run: five NLLB-signal languages chosen with FLORES+ devtest.** Kabuverdianu, Kabiye, Nuer, Bambara and Kabyle received the NLLB pseudo-reference signal because the rule ("teacher minus student at least +2 chrF++ on devtest, interval above 0") was applied to FLORES+ devtest scores. The final run's devtest results in those languages are selected on the test set.
5. **The science stage: no devtest in the hold-out.** The replay of original SFT windows (65% of the first mixture and 43% of A2W by tokens) was filtered against AfriScience dev and test, AfriLongDoc, the clean general dev set and FLORES+ dev English, but not against FLORES+ devtest, NTREX-128, MAFAND-MT test, SmolSent, SALT or GlotStoryBook. A UniMax draw of 9-12.5 M tokens is 0.4-0.6% of the windows, so about 0.4-0.6% of the roughly 8,300 FLORES+-carrying windows, a handful (an estimate from the sample).

*Selection and monitoring: development sets only.* The 760-sentence dev set that monitored training is 40 rows per language of a 10,043-row file (1,190 FLORES+ dev and 8,853 MAFAND dev rows, no devtest; which of the two sources the 40 come from is UNKNOWN). The headline RL-final is the last step (1,433), not the dev-best (step 986: 41.977 against 41.883 for the last step, a flat curve between 41.567 and 41.977), so selection by dev played no role for it; the interpolation weight, the mixture and the stopping rule of the final run used AfriScience dev, the general dev set and the weak-language dev set (FLORES+ dev, 60 sentences per language). FLORES+ devtest informed four decisions that do not change RL-final: the NLLB-language rule above, the decoding study (Section 6.5), informal increases of the draw weights of weak languages for the final run (a bet, not a measured effect), and the choice of RL-final over the final run as the headline model. ### 6.10 Long-context benchmark: does the model translate 2,048-token documents better than other systems?

Our working hypothesis during the project was that *our 270M model translates documents of up to about 2,048 tokens far better than other models, which do not support such inputs*. Sections 6.4 and 6.6 already weakened it for the RL model; this subsection tests it directly against other systems on one benchmark (agent LC1, `reports/long_context_benchmark_2026-10-23.md`). **The hypothesis is only partly supported, and the part that holds is narrower than the sentence above.** We state the test as a result, not as a claim of the model.

*Benchmark (LongCtx-MT, {{tref:t_longctx_benchmark}}).* Items are document times language. Real documents of the AfriLongDoc core (about 120 to 4,100 tokens, 12 languages, not in our training data) with the NLLB-200-3.3B sentence-by-sentence translation as a pseudo-reference; the human-reference SmolDoc documents (short ones and unrelated documents *stitched* to about 512, 1,024 and 2,048 tokens; 12 languages, 6 of which are common to all systems: hau yor swh amh zul ibo); and the AfriScience-MT dev and test papers (about 900 tokens, 6 languages, human references). Systems: our five checkpoints (SFT-final, RL-final, A2W, the step-250 checkpoint of the final run, and the final checkpoint of that run, step 401, added on 2026-10-24 with the same scripts, documents, languages and settings), NLLB-200 distilled-600M and 3.3B (inputs truncated at their 1,024 positions; their model card says they are not intended for document translation), TranslatePsy-AfriSLM 0.8B and 2B (262,144 context), and Qwen3.5-0.8B and Gemma-3-1B-it zero-shot. Our models generate the whole document natively, in one pass, and that is the mode they were trained for; sentence by sentence is a wrapper (split the source, translate each sentence, join) that we added to compare with systems that cannot take long input and as a repair route for unfinished sentences. Every system is run in two modes: the *whole document in one pass*, and *sentence by sentence* (for our models with the best-of-four repair of unfinished sentences of Section 6.4; for the others plain). Metrics are document-pooled chrF++ and spBLEU, finished rate, loop rate, source-sentence coverage (the alignment of the long-document reward), length ratio and GlotLID share. Reductions forced by the shared GPU: the NLLB whole-document runs cover six languages, TranslatePsy-0.8B seven, TranslatePsy-2B five languages with fewer bins; Gemma-3-4B-it, TranslatePsy-4B, AfriNLLB and Qwen3.5-2B were not run.

*Caveats.* (i) A2W and the final run (whose step-250 checkpoint is evaluated here) were trained on the AfriScience-MT *training* split, which advantages them on the AfriScience sets, and the run's checkpoint-selection rule uses the AfriScience dev set (the step-250 checkpoint evaluated here is simply the one that was current when the evaluation was started), so its dev column is not independent (its test column was not used for selection but is treated as dev-like); the SmolDoc and core documents are outside all training data (exact and 8-gram check). (i-b) The final checkpoint (step 401) was added to this benchmark on 2026-10-24, after the other systems, by running the same scripts on the same documents with the same decoding (vLLM, greedy, 3.5 times the source tokens; GPU memory fraction 0.25 instead of 0.07, which changes the batching but not the greedy decoding in principle) and the same scoring and bootstrap; the existing rows were re-aggregated by the same code and reproduce exactly. Its sentence-level chrF++ on the AfriScience papers (6 languages, sentence-by-sentence mode) is 48.06 / 47.26 (dev / test) against 48.05 / 47.26 for the step-250 checkpoint here and 48.12 / 47.30 from the separate evaluation script of Section 6.7, which also checks the harness. The step-250 and step-401 checkpoints differ by 151 RL steps on a plateau, there is one run per checkpoint and the document bootstrap contains no seed-to-seed variance, so the benchmark has little power to separate them. (ii) Stitched documents are unrelated texts joined, so they measure length robustness, not discourse; no human judge of discourse quality was used, and the independent LLM judge of Section 6.12 scores aligned units of one to a few sentences, not discourse. (iii) Document-pooled chrF++ forgives clause shifts of the sentence alignment and lies about 10 points above the sentence-level scores of the same output; it is not comparable with published sentence-level numbers. (iv) The numbers differ slightly from those of Section 6.4 because the document selection (20 documents per bin, 6 common languages) and the decoding budget (3.5 times the source tokens, greedy) differ, with the same conclusions; likewise the step-250 checkpoint finishes 96% of the AfriScience dev papers here against the 1.000 of Section 6.7 (the budget differs). (v) One checkpoint per system and one decoding setting; models above 8 GB were not run; whether the pretraining of NLLB, TranslatePsy, Qwen or Gemma contains SmolDoc or AfriScience is unknown (TranslatePsy lists Smol among its evaluation sets). (vi) Coverage and loop metrics use our own alignment and loop detector, which was designed for the reward and may disadvantage other systems' outputs slightly; the NLLB sentence-mode loop rate is inflated by repeated list items.

**Wording we use.** Whole-document decoding beyond about 512 tokens fails for NLLB (input truncated at 1,024 tokens), for TranslatePsy-AfriSLM (repetition loops) and for our SFT and RL checkpoints. After the science stage the step-250 and step-401 checkpoints of our 270M model finish 91-100% of the documents of 1,000-2,000 tokens and write the target language (chrF++ 39.6 and 28.2 / 27.7 against human references at about 1,024 and 2,048 tokens, A2W 39.4 and 28.8; on the AfriScience-MT test papers 41.5 / 41.3), where the NLLB and TranslatePsy systems we tested score 5.9-14.9 (AfriScience test 8.4-17.9), but they omit 12-30% of the sentences. A sentence-by-sentence wrapper removes the failure for every system and gives higher scores (52.6-58.7 chrF++, ours 52.6 and 55.5, the same for the step-250 and final checkpoints) than native long input, with NLLB and TranslatePsy 1-4 chrF++ ahead of ours. Better on native long input is therefore not better document translation, and we do not claim that our model translates long documents better than other models. An independent LLM judge applied to aligned units of the same core documents (Section 6.12) agrees and is sharper about quality: the whole-document units of the final checkpoint score 25.8 of 100 (16.4% of the sampled sentences without output), against 67.0 for its sentence-by-sentence wrapper and 85.6 and 86.5 for NLLB-200 3.3B and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B (five languages; whole minus wrapper -42.0 [-45.1, -38.8]), falling from 33.8 at about 1,024 tokens to 12.1 at about 2,048; the judge is validated only at Spearman about 0.5 and the whole-document units are blocks of 1.4 sentences, but these gaps are far larger than that noise. The chrF++ numbers and the judge tell consistent stories of different size: chrF++ against the pseudo-reference is much less harsh (the wrapper 1-4 chrF++ behind NLLB and TranslatePsy) than the judge (19.9 points behind NLLB, six languages), and the model finishes long documents and writes the right language, but what it writes is judged far below the wrapper and far below NLLB-200 3.3B; this is not specific to the Ge'ez-script languages (Section 6.12).

*An optional second pseudo-reference.* Because the NLLB pseudo-reference is biased and absent for 22 of the 23 consensus-only languages, LC1 prepared, but did not run, a Gemini Batch-API pseudo-reference; the plan and its costs are in Appendix I. No API call was made for that pseudo-reference (the LLM-judge calls of Section 6.12 are a separate use of the API).


### 6.11 Running the model on a laptop CPU

The model is small enough to run without a GPU, and the question a user asks is what it costs in quality and time. We measured this for the final checkpoint (`final2-step401`) converted to llama.cpp GGUF and to CTranslate2 (agent C1b, `reports/cpu_speed_2026-10-23.md`, data in `reports/data/cpu_speed_2026-10-23/`). **The measurements were made on a shared server, not on a laptop**: an Intel Xeon Gold 6126 (2.6 GHz, Skylake, AVX-512) pinned to exactly four cores under `nice`, three repetitions, while the ablation and evaluations of the project ran on the same machine (one to three busy neighbour cores). The headline numbers are *solo* runs; runs with several benchmarks in parallel were 7-17% slower ({{tref:t_cpu_speed}}; `solo_vs_parallel_streams.csv`) and rows measured that way are labelled "shared box". Quality is chrF++ against human references on 1,170 FLORES+ devtest sentences of six languages (Swahili, Hausa, Yoruba, Kinyarwanda, Amharic, Tigrinya; rows whose reference pair was not in the SFT text; 200 sentences each, 170 for Hausa), generated greedily; Tigrinya stands in for Nigerian Pidgin, which FLORES+ does not contain. The prompts were checked to match the Hugging Face chat template token for token (`prompt_mismatch_vs_hf_template` = 0 in `lean_rss.csv`).

*Quantisation costs almost nothing down to 8 bits.* {{tref:t_cpu_quant}} lists macro chrF++ and its difference to the bf16 GPU output (paired bootstrap over sentences). GGUF Q8\_0 is indistinguishable from the GPU (-0.02 chrF++, [-0.22, +0.16]) although only 18% of its outputs are identical strings, because greedy decoding diverges at near-ties without changing the score; CTranslate2 fp32 is level too (+0.03). *Why is a 270M model not faster than NLLB-600M?* The files give a measured part and leave a hypothesis. **Measured:** per sentence our model generates about 59-60 tokens against about 44 for NLLB (`gen_tok_mean` in `quality.csv`: 60.4 for Q8\_0, 59.1 for int8, 43.7 for NLLB-600M greedy), that is 1.35 times more output tokens, because the Gemma tokenizer fragments these languages more (Section 3.3). The generated tokens per second (end to end, batch 32) are about equal between our CTranslate2 int8 model (172) and NLLB-600M greedy (175), and lower for Q8\_0 (118). So the 1.45 times lower sentence rate of our int8 model is explained by the token count (1.35 times) and not by a slower step, but a model with less than half the parameters is also not faster per generated token. **Hypothesis, not measured:** 168M of our 270M parameters are the vocabulary table, which is also the output head, so every decoding step computes scores for 262,145 entries (about 168M multiply-adds against roughly 100M for the 18 layers), and CPU decoding at small batch is bound by memory traffic over all weights, which is consistent with the rise from 0.97 to 2.00 sentences per second between batch 1 and 32; the per-token cost of the 270M model would then be dominated by the head and not much lower than that of a larger model with a smaller head. We did not profile the time share of the output projection, and we did not count the output projection of NLLB-600M, which may be large too; the restricted-vocabulary idea of Section 3.3 (TODO(vocab)) would test it. The two token-rate figures also come from different sentence subsets and token definitions, so the equality is approximate.

*Estimate for a laptop (ESTIMATE, not a measurement).* We have no laptop measurement. The report assumes 0.5-1.0 times the measured Q8\_0 speed on an 8 GB, four-core laptop, because the Xeon core is probably faster than an older laptop core, laptop memory bandwidth (DDR4) lowers decode speed, and Apple performance cores may be faster; the factor is a judgement and not a result. With it, Q8\_0 would translate 1-2 sentences per second, that is 8-17 minutes per 1,000 sentences and 6-12 days per million: fine for thousands to tens of thousands of sentences, not for millions. For comparison the GPU (vLLM) was about 100 times faster in the same project.

*Caveats.* Shared machine and some one-repetition or shared-box rows (hybrid, fp32, Q4\_K\_M, the PyTorch rows); small speed subsets (120 sentences per timing, 20 paragraphs; Hausa has 170 clean sentences instead of 200); six languages, one of them a stand-in; one checkpoint (the final checkpoint, not the headline RL-final model; the speed does not depend on the weights, the quality numbers do); FLORES-length sentences only; no energy or battery measurement; no human evaluation. No TranslatePsy data or outputs were used.


### 6.12 An independent LLM judge: validation, FLORES+ and long documents

*Why, and what this section is.* Every learned metric used above is either part of the reward stack or shares data and encoders with it (Section 6.9), and chrF++ rewards surface overlap. A large language model prompted as a judge is a scorer that neither we nor the metric authors trained. The validation pilot of Section 7.1a showed that such a judge (gemini-3.8-flash) tracks human adequacy ratings of *other* systems about as well as SSA-COMET with reference (Spearman about 0.5); with the owner's approval we then validated the reference-free prompt and applied the judge to our models in three runs (2026-10-25): **(1)** validation of the reference-free prompt, **(2)** FLORES+ translations of SFT-final and RL-final, **(3)** long documents, judged on aligned units. The judge is a model and not a human, it is validated only at Spearman about 0.5, and **none of this is human evaluation**; we read only language-level or pooled differences, and, in Run 3, differences that are far larger than that noise.

*Protocol.* The prompts are Figure 8 (reference-based) and Figure 9 (reference-free, "QE") of the SSA-COMET paper [@li2025ssacomet], verbatim (Appendix J; the only change is that "[67]Most" is written "[67] Most"), with five demonstrations, one per adequacy interval (SSA-MTE train split for Amharic, Hausa, Kinyarwanda and Yoruba; other rows of the same AfriMTE-WMT2024 pair, scores min-max scaled, for Swahili, Igbo, Somali and Xhosa; the demonstrations are those of the pilot), score only, gemini-3.8-flash as served on 2026-10-25, temperature 0, thinking at the model default, Batch API, **one run per request** (nothing resubmitted). Figure 9 is Figure 8 with the `Reference:` field removed from the demonstrations and the test line (a unit test asserts this on all 300 pilot segments). The output cap was 1,000 tokens including thinking (1,500 for Run 1); the largest thinking budget used was 912 tokens, so the cap was never reached, and **none of the 6,190 responses failed to parse** (no non-STOP finish). The three runs cost USD 5.42 (the pilot of Section 7.1a USD 1.82 more; {{tref:t_judge_j2_cost}}), under a guard that refused any submit whose worst case would take the running total above USD 8. All judge scores below are on a 0-100 scale.

*Why Gemini-3.8-Flash, and not the judge of the SSA-COMET paper.* Li et al. [@li2025ssacomet] judge with Gemini-2.5-Pro. For our API key that model returned "no longer available to new users" (Batch API and standard API), so we did not run it, and the judge used here is not a reproduction of theirs; we do not claim that Flash is better than 2.5 Pro. In the pilot of Section 7.1a we compared Gemini-3.1-Pro-preview (Google's named successor of 2.5 Pro) with Gemini-3.8-Flash on the same 300 SSA-MTE and AfriMTE segments (one run per judge): Flash reaches a pooled Spearman correlation with the human z-score of 0.511 [0.41, 0.60] against 0.454 [0.35, 0.55] for 3.1 Pro; the paired difference Pro minus Flash is -0.057 [-0.09, -0.02], and its per-language intervals include zero in every language except Hausa and Somali; Flash costs about one fifth as much (USD 0.29 against 1.53 for the 300 segments). We therefore used Flash: on this sample it was not worse than the stronger model we could run, at a fifth of the price.

*Run 1: is the reference-free prompt usable?* On the 300 pilot segments the QE prompt reaches a pooled Spearman correlation with the human z-score of **0.494 [0.394, 0.586]**, against 0.511 [0.41, 0.60] for the reference-based prompt of the pilot, 0.536 [0.44, 0.62] for SSA-COMET-MTL with reference and 0.528 [0.43, 0.61] for SSA-COMET-MTL-QE (without reference); per-pair means removed 0.552 [0.46, 0.65], 0.584, 0.556 and 0.552. The paired differences are not distinguishable from zero (QE minus reference prompt, pooled: -0.017 [-0.048, +0.013]; per-pair means removed -0.033 [-0.056, +0.002]; QE judge minus SSA-COMET-MTL-QE, pooled: -0.034 [-0.117, +0.053]; no per-pair Spearman difference to the reference prompt excludes zero), and the scores of the two prompts correlate at 0.954 (Spearman; mean score 80.6 with, and 78.7 without, the reference). By the rule "usable if the confidence interval of the correlation excludes values below 0.2" the reference-free judge is usable for Amharic, Hausa, Kinyarwanda, Yoruba, Swahili and Igbo (**Kinyarwanda and Igbo only just**, lower bounds 0.203 and 0.214) and **not usable for Somali (lower bound -0.03) and Xhosa (-0.21)**, which were not used with the QE prompt ({{tref:t_judge_j2_validation}}). The six long-document languages pooled give 0.517 [0.414, 0.612]. The published Gemini-2.5-Pro figure for the QE prompt (average Spearman 0.610) is over the paper's own pairs, which include easier ones, and is not comparable with ours.

*How much of it is looping, and how much is the alignment?* The loop and unfinished flags of LC1 explain only part of the gap: 17.5% of the final checkpoint's sampled sentences lie in flagged documents (67.8% for RL-final), where the judged-unit mean is 8.9 (RL-final 4.2) against 27.3 (28.6) in unflagged documents; even outside the flagged documents the whole-document units score 27-29, still far below the wrapper (65.8 for its unflagged documents). Whole-document units that cover two or more sentences score lower (final checkpoint 4.6, against 29.9 for single-sentence units; RL-final 4.8 against 12.5), but block size is confounded with the failure mode (an output that drifts produces long blocks), so this does not by itself say that block-level judging lowers the score. The alignment diagnostic (an own character n-gram score, diagnostic only) finds that only 4.5% of the final checkpoint's units (2.2% of RL-final's, 0.2% of TranslatePsy's, none of the wrapper's and NLLB's) match a shifted block clearly better than their own block (their judge mean is 1.7, so removing them would move the mean by about 1 point), and the judge score correlates with the own-block score at 0.74 (final checkpoint, whole document; RL-final 0.57); within the window of three sentences the low scores are not an alignment artefact, but the check cannot see larger drifts or an unlucky bead boundary.

*What this adds to Section 6.10, and what it does not.* The evidence of Section 6.10 (chrF++ against human references and a pseudo-reference, finished rate, loop rate, coverage) said that native whole-document translation of the science-stage model finishes documents, writes the target language and omits 12-30% of the sentences, while a sentence wrapper gives every system more. The independent judge says the same, and more sharply about *quality*: the text that the final checkpoint does write in whole-document mode is judged far below its own sentence-by-sentence translation and far below NLLB-200 and TranslatePsy, and it gets worse from 1,024 to 2,048 tokens. We therefore keep the existing statements (finishes, writes the language, beats the whole-document baselines on chrF++) and add that its judged quality is low; whole-document capability is "finishes and writes the language", not "translates well". **The two kinds of number tell the same story at different sizes.** chrF++ against pseudo-references (Section 6.10) shows a model that finishes long documents and writes the right language, with the wrapper 1-4 chrF++ behind NLLB; the judge, once the output is aligned to the source sentences, says that what the model writes in whole-document mode is far worse than its own wrapper (25.8 against 67.0) and than NLLB-200 3.3B (85.6), and puts the wrapper itself 19.9 points behind NLLB (six languages). chrF++ against pseudo-references is much less harsh than the judge, and the two scales are not comparable (caveat 4); we report both and rely on neither alone.

*Is it the Ge'ez-script languages?* Amharic accounts for much of the wrapper's gap to NLLB (-50.3 points, against -5.7 to -25.8 in the other five languages), so {{tref:t_noGeez_judge}} gives the figures without it. The macro over the four non-Ge'ez languages common to all systems (Hausa, Yoruba, Swahili, Igbo; the same document-cluster bootstrap, seed and resampling weights as above, computed by `paper/scripts/make_noGeez.py`, which reproduces the stored five-language rows exactly) is **74.3 [72.4, 76.3] for the wrapper, 85.1 [83.6, 86.6] for NLLB-200 3.3B, 86.9 [85.6, 88.1] for TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B, 30.4 [26.1, 34.6] for the final checkpoint's whole-document output and 14.5 [10.9, 18.1] for RL-final's**. The wrapper-minus-NLLB difference on identical sentences is -10.9 [-12.7, -9.1] on these four languages (-18.8 [-20.5, -17.1] on the five with Amharic) and the wrapper-minus-TranslatePsy difference -12.6 [-14.3, -11.0] (-19.6 on five). Ge'ez script therefore explains part of the sentence-level gap, but not all of it: the wrapper is also far behind NLLB in Kinyarwanda (-25.8) and Yoruba (-19.5). **The whole-document failure is not a Ge'ez effect.** The final checkpoint's whole-document mean is 7.7 in Amharic, but also 9.0 in Yoruba, 14.6 in Kinyarwanda and 25.9 in Igbo, against 43.0 in Hausa and 43.6 in Swahili; whole document minus wrapper is -44.3 [-48.7, -40.0] on the four non-Ge'ez languages (-42.0 on all six) and is larger in Yoruba (-56.1) than in Amharic (-30.6); whole document minus NLLB-200 3.3B is -54.8 [-59.0, -50.6] on the four. We did not test why the model is further behind in the Ge'ez-script languages (script, tokenisation cost, little data, model capacity are all candidates) and make no claim about the cause; a specialist model and a larger model are future work (Section 11).

*Caveats.* (1) **Validated at about 0.5.** The judge correlates with human ratings of other systems' output at Spearman about 0.5; a language-level or pooled difference can be read, a single sentence cannot (Run 2: 0.30-0.55 agreement with the learned metrics). The Run 3 gaps (20-80 points) are far above that noise; the Run 2 gaps of 2-7 points are not, and the Hausa, Swahili, Igbo and Somali values rest on 25-50 validation segments per pair. (2) **Unit size and prompt.** The QE prompt was validated on single sentences (Run 1); Run 3 judges blocks of 1-4 sentences (mean 1.4) for the whole-document systems and single sentences for the wrapper and the baselines, so the whole-document minus wrapper contrast mixes block-level and sentence-level judging; we did not judge the wrapper outputs at block level. For Kinyarwanda and Igbo the lower validation bound is 0.20-0.21; Somali and Xhosa are not used. (3) **Alignment.** The aligner is the production Gale-Church aligner with omission beads; its pair F1 on corrupted outputs is 0.78 (macro; 0.34 for a text cut at half its length) and lower for loops and truncation (figures from the reward-v2 evaluation as quoted in the judge report, not recomputed here; Section 4.4 gives 0.89 for the anchored aligner on twelve other corruptions); the aligned unit of a drifting output is uncertain by construction. Omissions follow the LC1 definition (omitted or copied). (4) **A reference-free LLM judge is harsher than chrF++ against pseudo-references** (NLLB's own outputs are the pseudo-references of Section 6.10 and favour NLLB-like systems), and we did not test whether the judge itself prefers NLLB-like text. (5) **Sample sizes and sampling.** Run 3 has 100 sampled sentences per language and system with document-cluster intervals; Run 2 uses `no_pair` rows only and 170 sentences for Hausa. The NLLB-3.3B outputs of the core documents are the same sentence translations that serve as the LC1 pseudo-reference and as the length model of the aligner for every system. (6) One run of a hosted model that may change; no human reference for Run 3; whole-document modes of NLLB and TranslatePsy and the Gemini pseudo-reference of Appendix I were not judged. (7) **Overlap of the validation material with our training and evaluation text (Section 6.9a).** The validation used the SSA-MTE train split, which is in our SFT text, and AfriMTE-WMT2024, whose English sources are 100% FLORES+ devtest sentences (1,916 sentences, nine pairs). The judge scored other systems' outputs there, so our models' exposure does not enter the validation correlations, but the validation sentences are not independent of our training and evaluation text, and all 19 few-shot demonstrations of the Swahili, Igbo, Somali and Xhosa prompts are FLORES+ devtest sentences (with other systems' translations and human scores) from the benchmark that Run 2 samples (an index-based check found 5 of the 800 sampled sentences of these four languages among the demonstrations of their own prompts, about what chance gives; we did not verify the sentence-id mapping further). The demonstrations are identical for SFT-final and RL-final, so they cannot favour one of the two. (8) **Disclosure.** Run 3 judges the existing LC1 outputs of NLLB-200 3.3B and TranslatePsy-AfriSLM-0.8B; they were sent to the Gemini API **for evaluation only**, as were the benchmark sentences (SSA-MTE, AfriMTE, FLORES+, AfriLongDoc); no output of these systems and no TranslatePsy data was used for training or in any experiment, and judge outputs are used for evaluation only. All prompts and responses are stored with the results (`reports/data/judge_j2_2026-10-25/`).

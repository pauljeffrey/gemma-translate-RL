### 4.1 GRPO as used here

We use group-relative policy optimisation [@shao2024deepseekmath] with a single on-policy update per sampled batch (so the importance ratio is exactly one and no clipping is needed; this is asserted in the code at every step). For a prompt $x$ with target language $\ell$ the policy samples a group of $G$ completions $y_1,\dots,y_G$ at temperature 1, top-$p$ 1, top-$k$ disabled, so that the sampling distribution is the scored distribution. Each completion receives a scalar reward $r_i \in [-2, 2.25]$ (Section 4.3). The advantage is the group-standardised reward multiplied by a per-language weight $w_\ell$ that reflects how reliable the content signal of that language is,
$$A_i = w_\ell \, \frac{r_i - \bar r}{\sigma_r + 10^{-4}},$$
with $A_i=0$ for every member of a group whose standard deviation is below $10^{-6}$. The loss is the token-level policy-gradient term plus $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}$ to a frozen reference, normalised by the number of completion tokens of the whole optimiser step. The KL term uses the $k_3$ estimator, clamped at a log-ratio of 5 ($k_{3c}$, Section 4.5); $\beta=0.05$ in phases 1-2c and 0.1 in the final run. Optimisation is AdamW (betas 0.9 / 0.95, no weight decay, gradient clip 1.0, fp32 master weights, bf16 autocast) at learning rates of 1e-6 to 3e-6 ({{tref:t_hparams}} in the appendix). Rollouts use vLLM; the trainer checks every 25 steps that engine and trainer log-probabilities agree.

**Prompts.** RL prompts are English windows of consecutive whole sentences from one document (English Wikipedia, FineWeb-Edu), filtered for readability, technical vocabulary, digits, markup and repetition (the scorer is weakest on dense scientific English), in length bins named by their upper bound in AfroXLM-R tokens: b64, b256, b512, b1024 and, from phase 2c, b2048 (windows of 1,025-2,048 tokens made of up to 183 sentences). There are 77 language files; the draw weights favour the weakest languages (1.5-4.0 against 1.0; 2.5-4.0 in phase 2c) and a floor guarantees every language at least two prompts in every block of 20 steps. The final run draws 70% of its prompts from science documents (AfriScience-MT training side, PubMed, arXiv, LibreTexts) and 30% from the general pools.

### 4.2 Length curriculum

The bins are trained in stages, b64 then b256, b512, b1024, then (phase 2c) b2048, and finally a random mixture of all bins. Phase 1 used 40, 60, 60 and 60 steps for the first four stages, then the mixture. Phase 2c, restarted from the b64-stage model of phase 2b, used 391 steps of b256, 446 of b512, 149 of b1024, 298 of b2048 and 149 of the mixture (1,433 steps, about 900,000 sampled completions). Per step the number of prompts shrinks with the length (64 / 48 / 42 / 42 / 21 / 42 prompts for b64 to mixed, each with 16 completions), and the token budget grows with it (384 new tokens for b64 up to 7,168 for b2048 and the mixture, 3.5 times the source length). The curriculum is trained with the *chunked* reward below, so a long output is scored sentence by sentence even though it is generated in one pass.

### 4.3 The reward

The reward is the central engineering contribution of this work. Its structure is in {{fref:fig1_pipeline}}B; this section gives the logic, Appendix C the formulas and calibration facts.

**Sanitisation and hard failures.** The completion is cut at the first stop token, decoded, scrubbed of special-token strings, NFC-normalised and stripped of the language tag; only this text reaches any scorer. A completion that does not end inside the budget, writes anything after the end token, contains a turn marker, has a missing or wrong tag, is empty, copies the source (word-set Jaccard of at least 0.9), is degenerate (one word repeated more than four times in a row, fewer than 30% distinct words), has a character length ratio outside [0.2, 5] (lower bound 0.4 for languages without a human length window), or is classified by GlotLID [@kargaran2023glotlid] as another language receives the floor reward $-2$ and is never scored. The GlotLID guard is enabled for 75 of the 77 languages (those whose measured recall on human text is at least 0.90; off for Pidgin and Kongo), with sibling labels accepted where the classifier confuses close languages and a minimum text length per language.

**Chunking.** Source and output are split into sentences and aligned: if the counts are equal the $i$-th pair is scored, otherwise a language-aware Gale-Church alignment [@gale1993program] with constant-cost omission and extra-sentence beads (the length model comes from the language's human references, not from the pair itself) pairs them. An omitted sentence is a *failed chunk* (reward $-2$, weighted by its source characters); an unmatched extra output sentence or text appended beyond the language's length window is a failed chunk weighted by its output characters. Without the language-aware cost the legacy aligner found an omission bead in only 2 of 20,000 synthetic trials (0% sentence recall); with it, 56% of dropped sentences, 77% of documents and 89% of dropped characters are found at 0.6% false alarms on seven of eight languages, and extra sentences go from 42% to 93% ({{tref:t_anchors}} and Appendix C).

**Content signal.** For the 42 validated languages and the 12 blend languages the content signal is reference-free quality estimation. We started with AfriCOMET-QE [@wang2024africomet] and, after a study of agreement with human judgments (Section 7.1), replaced it for the final run by an *ensemble* with SSA-COMET-MTL run without reference [@li2025ssacomet]. For each sentence pair the two scores are shaped separately into rewards $r_1,r_2$ (below) and combined as
$$r = \begin{cases} \tfrac12 (r_1 + r_2) & |r_1 - r_2| \le \delta \\ \min(r_1, r_2) & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad \delta = 1.0,$$
so that a chunk that only one judge likes (the signature of copying the English source, which the joint-encoder SSA-COMET-MTL rewards) earns the lower of the two. This is a design choice tested on corrupted references (Section 7.2); its effect on the trained policy was not measured (Section 7.9). For the 23 consensus-only languages, and with weight 0.5 for the 12 blend languages, the content signal is the *consensus*: the mean sentence chrF++ of a completion against the other valid completions of its own group, mapped linearly onto the reward range ($-2 + 4.25\cdot\mathrm{chrF}/100$). Groups with fewer than three valid members are skipped; near-loops are kept out of the pool by a self-repetition guard. The final run adds, for five covered weak languages (Kabuverdianu, Kabiye, Nuer, Bambara, Kabyle), a signal from pseudo-references produced by NLLB-200 3.3B (weight 0.23-0.35); see Section 7.5 for why this is restricted.

**Shaping.** A raw QE score is mapped per language onto a piecewise-linear, monotone curve with three anchors measured on human references of that language: the reward is 0 at the 90th percentile of the score of *mismatched* human references (the failure line), $+1$ at the median of the score of matched human references and $+2$ at their 90th percentile, with only a further +0.25 in total between that point and a score of 1 ("a score above what human translations get is where a learned metric is gamed"). The reward range is therefore $[-2, +2.25]$ and a translation at the level of a human median earns $+1$. Each language also receives an advantage weight in $[0, 1]$ derived from how reliably its QE score ranks the human reference above corrupted versions of the same source.

**Penalties.** Subtracted from the shaped chunk reward and floored at $-2$: a length-window and a word-ratio penalty against the human distribution of the language; a lexical penalty (numbers and names in the source that disappear, content-word precision); a diacritic penalty (density of tone marks and special letters below the human 10th percentile, or foreign, orphan, unseen or over-decorated marks); and, for the final run, the *anchors* of Section 7.3 (substring copy of the source, graded entity loss, function-word profile, padding by type/token ratio, repetition and a cross-prompt boilerplate buffer). Every threshold of every penalty is a quantile of *human* text (for RL-final the dev references of FLORES+ and MAFAND, and for Efik the IbomMT *test* set; for the anchors of the final run also the test splits of six benchmarks: Section 6.9a) and every penalty is a penalty only, never a bonus: a bonus for retaining numbers or names would push towards copying. The anchors are a design motivated by detection tests on corrupted human text (Section 7.3). What they did in the final run is reported in Section 7.9: no demonstrated protective effect for the copy, entity and function-word terms (RL-final did not show these failures), padding and repetition flags 2-4 pp lower than RL-final in the weak and consensus-only languages, and no control run from the same start with the plain reward.

**Aggregation.** The reward of a completion is the source-length-weighted mean of its chunk rewards, in which failed chunks count as $-2$.

### 4.4 Reward v2 for long documents

For sources of at least 150 words the final run uses a second reward (Section 7.4). The v1 rule that flags a document as degenerate when fewer than 30% of its words are distinct fires on length alone (it flags 47% of good sentence-by-sentence translations at b4096), so v2 replaces it by a continuous multi-level repetition *severity*: word 4-gram repeat share, share of words in repeated word 8-grams, duplicated sentences, repeated blocks and periodic word cycles, each measured as the excess over the same share of the English source and ramped between the 99.5th percentile of human documents of that length bucket and full degeneracy. A stuttering sentence is a failed chunk, not a failed document. Alignment uses numbers and names as anchors and removes redundant copies first (pair F1 0.89 against 0.66 for the legacy aligner over twelve corruptions). The sentence rewards are aggregated by the mean minus the tail deficit beyond what human documents show (`cvar_excess`), and a coverage term lowers the reward towards the failure value as the failed share of the source grows. Section 7.9 reports what the final run achieved with it: whole-document output far better than RL-final's, of which about three quarters were already in place after the science stage and before this reward, a rise of severe repetition from step 250 to the end of the run, and no control run with reward v1.

### 4.5 Stability: the divergence of phase 2b, and the guards

Phase 2b (lr 3e-6, group of 16 with one greedy member, *no* division by the group standard deviation, $k_3$ KL with coefficient 0.05) ran smoothly in its b64 stage, with a KL of 0.03-0.04 to the reference and a gradient norm of 0.5-0.7, and diverged when the b256 stage began at step 489: the mean KL rose from 0.04 to 0.27 at step 541, 1.8 at step 661 and 2.6 at step 781, the finished rate of the evaluation prompts fell from 0.95 to 0.56, and we stopped the run at step 839 ({{fref:fig3_phase_diagnostics}}). Sentence-level dev chrF++ stayed at 41.9-42.0 until step 700 and fell to 41.4 only at step 750: the metric that selects checkpoints noticed the divergence last. Phase 2c restarted from the step-488 model with three changes made together: standardised advantages (as in phase 2), a lower learning rate of 2e-6, and a *KL guard* that halves the learning rate (down to 1/8) when the mean KL of the last five steps exceeds 0.15. We did not run ablations that isolate which change fixed the instability; the greedy group member was also removed in 2c, and later measurements show that a looping greedy completion of several thousand tokens produces gradient norms of 8-19 (against 0.5 without it) because all its tokens push in the same direction (`reports/final_run_integration_2026-10-21.md` section 5), so it is a candidate cause as well.

Later in 2c two single steps showed KL values of 0.51 (step 607) and 1,651 (step 747) with gradient norms of 220 and 285,381: the unbounded $k_3$ estimator $e^{d}-d-1$ explodes for a sampled token whose probability the policy has pushed far below the reference. With the gradient clip at 1.0 such a step is effectively discarded, but the guard cut the learning rate after step 747 although nothing had diverged. We therefore clamp the log-ratio at 5 ($k_{3c}$, identical below the clamp, no gradient beyond it) and compute the guard from the median instead of the mean of its window; both were applied from the b2048 restart at step 900.

### 4.6 Science stage and weight interpolation

Between the general RL (step 1,433) and the final run we added a short supervised stage on the AfriScience-MT corpus [@abdulmumin2026afriscience] (six African languages, simplified science text, 5,792 training sentences per language) mixed with replay of the general human references, self-distilled "anchor" examples, the best translations saved by the RL archive and *long-document distillation*: documents translated sentence by sentence by the RL model with a best-of-four choice by the QE ensemble and joined into one target, so that the model learns to translate a whole document and to stop (13.5% of the chosen mixture). The stage has about 320 steps at 3e-6. Because plain fine-tuning lowered the weak-blend languages, the result is *interpolated* with the RL model, $\theta=(1-\alpha)\theta_{\text{RL}}+\alpha\theta_{\text{SFT-stage}}$ [@wortsman2022robust], and the mixture and $\alpha$ were chosen by guardrails (Section 6.6): best sentence-level chrF++ on the AfriScience dev set such that the general dev set, every weak-language group and the paper-level finished rate do not get worse.

### 4.7 The final run (final2)

The final run starts from the chosen interpolated model (mixture "A2W", $\alpha=0.7$) with the KL reference set to that model, 700 planned steps (the run was wound down after step 350 and ended at step 401, Section 6.7) at learning rate 1e-6 (20 warm-up steps), 16 completions per group of which one is greedy, with the greedy member's advantage computed from the statistics of the sampled members only, capped at 3 and scaled by 0.3, the KL guard, the QE ensemble, the v2/v3 anchors, the long-document reward v2, the NLLB signal for five languages, the language floor and a static per-language draw-weight list (Appendix B, {{tref:t_hparams}}). A chrF-informed adaptive language-weight controller exists but ran in dry-run mode only (it logged proposals and changed nothing). The run had a planned restart at step 150 and was OOM-killed twice on 2026-10-23 and resumed from its step-250 checkpoint, with the learning-rate scale raised from 0.5 to 0.75 and the KL guard reset (Section 6.7); it ended at step 401 after a 50-step linear learning-rate wind-down.

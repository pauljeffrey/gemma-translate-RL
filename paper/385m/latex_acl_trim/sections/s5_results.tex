\subsection{How does the model compare with other systems?}\label{sec:compare}
% [FULL = reports/full_results_comparison_2026-11-06.md; N2 NLLBRL; E-03 control; numbers from tabA/tabA2/tabD of paper_385m/latex_snippets_full_results]
\textbf{Answer: the RL model beats the SFT baseline everywhere, but it stays below the NLLB systems.} On identical FLORES+ sentences it scores about one chrF++ point below NLLB-600M and about three below NLLB-3.3B, which is about 92\% of the larger system's score (Table~\ref{tab:full-core}, Figure~\ref{fig:full}). Held-out AfriCOMET gives the same order with a wider gap. The RL model is ahead of NLLB-600M in only some languages and of NLLB-3.3B in a few (last column). NTREX and MAFAND-MT show the same order against NLLB-3.3B; there, only language-level NLLB averages exist, so these rows have no sentence pairing and no AfriCOMET.

The 19 languages that TranslatePsy-0.8B covers show the limits of the comparison. TranslatePsy-0.8B is the strongest system there and clearly ahead of the RL model. It was distilled from NLLB-3.3B outputs, so its lead is partly a student--teacher effect and not independent evidence of a better design. The SFT-only control, which was trained on NLLB-3.3B translations for these languages, matches the RL model in chrF++ but not on the neural score. We decode greedily and NLLB with beam search (four beams); on a 20-language check, beam search or MBR moved the RL model by less than one chrF++ point either way (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). SmolSent, SALT and three smaller benchmarks have no RL model; their per-language results are in Appendix~\ref{app:fullresults}.

\subsection{RQ1: How much does RL improve translation?}\label{sec:rq1}
% [R1 R2 R3 EVID tables 1-2]
\textbf{Answer: a lot, for every language we test.} The RL model beats the SFT baseline on all three benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). Every language on FLORES+ and NTREX improves, and so does every MAFAND-MT language except Nigerian Pidgin, which does not change measurably. The gain is large where the baseline was weakest. On FLORES+ the baseline leaves about one output in six unfinished and repeats itself in about one in five; the RL model almost always finishes and rarely repeats (Table~\ref{tab:headline}, lower rows).

\textbf{Where RL does not help.} For Arabic dialects, Tamazight, Dinka, Tamasheq and Kanuri, quality stays very low (chrF++ below about 14) and RL makes one problem worse: the model increasingly answers in English. For Tunisian Arabic, English output rises from about one fifth of outputs to almost half (Appendix~\ref{app:degenapp}). These languages were not among the RL prompts.

\subsection{RQ2: Is the gain just finishing and not repeating?}\label{sec:rq2}
% [R4 R5 R6 R13 R19-R24 D5 D7 E01 E02 E09b]
\textbf{Answer: about half of it is, and a decoding setting recovers that half for the SFT baseline; RL still adds clearly more.} We tested this in three ways. First, we cut the baseline's output at the first repetition, which leaves the translation untouched; this recovers 49\% of the chrF++ gain. Second, we gave the baseline a repetition penalty during decoding, with no further training. It recovers the same 49\%. With the penalty on both models, RL is still about 4.4 chrF++ ahead (Table~\ref{tab:decoding}, Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}). This control is imperfect: the best penalty strength was at the edge of the range we searched, and stronger values looked better on a development set but were never evaluated fully, so the baseline may be under-tuned.

Third, we tried stronger decoding. Beam search and MBR help the baseline much more than they help the RL model. MBR gives the baseline a large boost and the RL model a small one; for beam search, the RL result depends on how the search decides to stop, so we draw no conclusion from it. Split by language tier (exploratory; our own grouping of the 20 languages by their NLLB-3.3B score, without tier-level intervals), MBR helps the SFT baseline most in the weakest tier (about 6.5 chrF++, against 4.6 in the strongest), while the RL model gains about 1 in every tier (Appendix~\ref{app:decoding}). RL with plain greedy decoding stays about 1.6 points ahead of the best decoded baseline, which needs about 17 times the computation per sentence. The beam-search numbers of an earlier study that used 256 output tokens are not comparable, and we do not use them (Appendix~\ref{app:decoding}).

\textbf{A caveat on our own checks.} The repetition flag we report is also part of the reward, so we re-measured with a detector that shares none of its code. It finds about 1.8 times as many repetitive outputs as the reward's flag does. Rescoring the extra outputs at their language's average changes the RL model's chrF++ by at most 0.2, so the conclusion stands (Appendix~\ref{app:degenapp}).

\subsection{RQ3: Does the same compute on more SFT do as well?}\label{sec:rq3}
% [R14 CTL7-CTL11] GATE E-03
\textbf{Answer: inconclusive by the rule we wrote down beforehand.} We continued SFT for the same GPU time as the three RL stages, on half replay data and half extra English-to-X pairs for 19 languages. The extra pairs are NLLB-3.3B translations of web-text sentences \citep{gritta2026translatepsy}. We fixed the decision rule, the main comparison and the language groups before looking at any benchmark result for the control. The rule had four conditions; three were met. The fourth, that RL must also beat the control on the 19 languages that had extra data, was not met. So by our rule the result is inconclusive, and we did not change the rule afterwards.

In plain terms (Table~\ref{tab:control}, Figure~\ref{fig:control}): on the 19 languages with extra data, the control matches RL in chrF++ and finishes its sentences almost as reliably; it is even ahead on the learned metrics there, which were partly used to filter its data. On the other languages, the control barely improves on the baseline, while RL improves them all. Taking only the overall number, RL looks better; taking only the 19 languages, the two look equal. Neither alone is the registered verdict.

\textbf{Caveats.} The control is one run with one learning rate and one stopping point; a different mix or a longer run could change the picture. It is also not data-matched: its extra pairs come from a larger system (NLLB-3.3B). We therefore claim neither that RL is a better use of compute than SFT nor the opposite.

\subsection{RQ4: How reliable is the gain, and how does the model compare with a larger system?}\label{sec:rq4}
% [R8 E04 E11 M10 R17 R18 N1-N6 NLLBRL]
\textbf{Answer: the gain is robust to random seeds, nearly all of it comes from the first RL stage, and the model reaches about 92\% of NLLB-3.3B's chrF++ on shared languages (Section~\ref{sec:compare}).} The first RL stage produces 94\% of the gain reached after the second stage, and the two later stages add little. Two extra random seeds of the first stage, run on 20 languages, differ by only 0.06 chrF++, far less than the gain itself. The last RL step is about the size of that seed noise, so we do not call it a gain (Figure~\ref{fig:seeds}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig16_e04_e11_seeds_ablations.pdf}
\caption{Seed noise and what four extra training features are worth (20 languages, chrF++ gain over the SFT baseline after 100 RL steps). (a) Two seeds of the full recipe (shaded band) and three runs that each remove a feature. (b) The same effects against the seed difference (shaded): removing all four features costs about 0.3 points, removing the beam-search guide is not separable from seed noise. Error bars: 95\% intervals; one run per arm.}\label{fig:seeds}
\end{figure*}

We also tested four features that we added during the first stage. Together they are worth about 0.3 chrF++ at 100 steps, mostly because of one of them, a term that rewards agreement with human reference translations for seven languages. A beam-search guide, which costs about a quarter more time per step, is not shown to be worth it, and none of the features causes the drift from the starting model.


NLLB-3.3B is about 8.6 times larger than our model, and Section~\ref{sec:compare} gives the comparison. The RL model closes about three quarters of the gap that the baseline leaves to NLLB-3.3B, and the remaining gap is not a matter of unfinished sentences. On the 43 languages shared with the published NLLB table the RL model scores about 92\% of NLLB-3.3B's chrF++ (Appendix~\ref{app:fullresults}). It matches or beats NLLB-3.3B on seven languages, all among those where NLLB scores lowest. Caveats: NLLB's published numbers use a slightly different test set and beam search; the hardest languages have no published NLLB number; and for a few languages the reward itself used NLLB outputs as a target. We therefore do not claim to beat NLLB. Compared with our earlier 270M model run in the same harness, the 385M SFT baseline is behind, the RL model is level on two benchmarks and behind on one, and it is behind at document level (Appendix~\ref{app:fullresults}).

\subsection{Further results: science text and long documents}\label{sec:science}
% [SC1-SC6 DL1-DL5 E05 E14 E15 E16]
\textbf{Answer: for science text, the extra fine-tuning step carries the gain, and whole-document translation still does not work.} A short fine-tuning step on science text raises chrF on AfriScience-MT but not the neural score the benchmark uses. An RL run started from that model adds a little more. To ask whether the science step is needed, we ran the same RL without it (Table~\ref{tab:sci}): it ends about 1.4 chrF below the model that had the science step (one run each), and it gains little over its own start. The neural quality score rates the two models as equal, although chrF and held-out AfriCOMET rate the no-science-step model lower; because that score belongs to the family of scores the RL optimises, we do not treat it as evidence of quality. Translating whole documents in one pass fails for all our models. RL on long inputs raises the share of finished documents from 70\% to 82\%, but about three quarters of the documents still contain loops, so we translate sentence by sentence (Appendix~\ref{app:docs}).

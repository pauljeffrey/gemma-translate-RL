## 6. One script for the new GPU instance
```bash
export HF_TOKEN=...            # read: openlanguagedata/flores_plus (gated), <HF_ORG_LEGACY>/sabiyarn-clean-sft-2026-10-28, <HF_ORG_LEGACY>/sabiyarn-clean-rl-prompts-2026-10-28; write only for --push

python data_pipeline/sft_clean/r4_run_all.py --work /workspace/calib_r4 --plan        # shows done / TODO per step and its dependencies
```
* Idempotent: every step writes `<work>/.done/<step>` and is skipped when done (`--force STEP` redoes one; `--only STEP ...` runs some; a QE part also skips every score file that exists). It refuses a `--work` inside the repository, checks the split-file sha256, tags everything derived from IbomMT `nc_exception: ibom-mt`, runs a leak scan (no H_cal / H_sel sentence of 25+ characters may appear in any output file) before copying the numbers to `gemma_mt/calibration/clean_2026-10-28/*_final.json` (+ `qe_work_numbers/`, `status_final.json`), and with `--push` uploads only those.
* **Concurrent** (a dependency graph, `--jobs 8`): the Hub streaming of the SFT shards (the long pole) runs while the GPU scores the references; `--qe-parts 2` starts 2 processes per model, so 4 QE processes share the 96 GB card, with batches of 512 (AfriCOMET) and 256 (SSA-COMET-MTL); the CPU refits (noqe, lid, funcwords, science, longdoc, efi) run alongside. Tested on the laptop: the scheduler (inputs and refs start together, the 4 QE parts start together after them), plan mode, the leak scan, noqe with the fixed cost, FLORES-only long-document limits. The Hub and GPU steps are NOT tested end to end.
* Updated TODO-on-GPU list with ESTIMATED minutes (RTX PRO 6000, $0.58/h): QE scoring of H_cal references 66k pairs about 4 min wall (1,100 pairs/s assumed, plus 2 min model load, both models and 4 processes in parallel); efi / ibb statistics 2 min; the CPU refits 15-20 min wall in parallel with the QE; SFT streaming 30-60 min (network bound, runs in parallel); anchors 10 min after everything else; total wall about 45-75 min, dominated by the SFT streaming, so about USD 0.45-0.75. To pay less, run `--only sft sftprep` once on any cheap CPU machine first (the outputs `sft_all.jsonl`, `sft_strict.jsonl`, `sft_targets.jsonl`, `fw_corpus/` are not benchmark text) and copy them into the work directory of the GPU instance. ## 9. Two plain-language paragraphs for the owner
**The diacritic penalty and the 9.0% to 11.2% figure.** In languages such as Yoruba, Fon, Ewe or Igbo, small marks above and below letters (tone marks, dots) change the word. The reward looks at what marks and letter-mark combinations real human translations of that language use, and gives a small deduction to a translation that uses combinations nobody writes or that has far fewer marks than people normally write. To test whether that guard is fair we ran it on human reference sentences the guard had not been tuned on and counted how often it wrongly complained about perfectly good human text. Before, about 9 in 100 good sentences were wrongly flagged; with the new, smaller list about 11 in 100. Two things make that rise look worse than it is: the old figure was flattering because the old list had been built from those very sentences, and part of the flagged sentences are human sentences that really carry no marks at all (about a quarter of the Yoruba reference sentences), which no list can fix. So 11% means "the penalty occasionally touches a good translation, mostly in the languages with many marks (Yoruba, Fon, Twi)", not "11% of the reward is wrong"; the deduction is small and cannot be gamed upward.

**What the minimum-count rule trades off.** When the list of allowed mark combinations was built, we had only 261 reference sentences per language instead of 997, so the list was too short and would have punished rare but valid combinations. We therefore added combinations seen in our training text, but only those seen at least 5 times. A higher number keeps typing slips, wrong encodings and noise from other sources out of the list, so the guard stays strict and still catches a model that garbles marks; the price is that a genuinely rare, correct combination is missing and a good translation using it gets a small deduction. A lower number (2) protects rare correct forms and lowers the false-complaint rate, but lets more noisy spellings through and makes the guard weaker. We kept 5 as the middle path; if the model later turns out to avoid rare correct combinations in the languages with many marks, lowering it to 2 or 3 is the first thing to try.
